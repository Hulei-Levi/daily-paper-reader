## ABSTRACT 

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models are increasingly deployed alongside Speculative Decoding (SD) to accelerate inference, but combining the two is challenging. SD improves the inference speed of dense models by verifying groups of tokens in parallel. However, the inference speedup for SD with MoEs depends heavily on the number of tokens being verified. Using more verification tokens results in more experts being transferred from DRAM to the Neural Processing Unit (NPU), which increases the memory transfer cost. This negatively impacts model runtime, as memory transfer is typically the bottleneck in inference. In this work, we investigate the impact of MoE router design during training on the speed of MoEs with SD. We find that routers with high degrees of expert coactivation result in much faster runtimes, mitigating the impact of using more verification tokens. Motivated by this observation, we assess the impact of various router design choices on expert coactivation and runtime using billion-parameter transformer models. We find that combining a global load-balancing loss, shared experts, a consistency loss, and an autoregressive expert selection mechanism during training results in significantly stronger expert coactivation. This increased coactivation translates into higher overall runtime throughput: our exploration yields a model that improves throughput by 21% over MoE baselines, while maintaining on-par accuracy with the baseline MoE. 1arXiv:2609.22471v1 [cs.LG] 18 Sep 2026 1 INTRODUCTION 

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models have demonstrated strong results in language-modeling tasks (Jacobs et al., 1991; Muennighoff et al., 2024; Shazeer et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2022). Their appeal lies in their ability to scale up model sizes while reducing training and inference cost by activating only a subset of the model’s feedforward network (FFN) parameters during the forward pass. This technique has become widely adopted among today’s leading open-weight LLMs (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025; Muennighoff et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025; OpenAI, 2025; NVIDIA, 2026; Xiaomi LLM-Core Team, 2026). In a transformer, MoE and dense models differ primarily in the FFN, where an MoE dynamically routes each token to a subset of expert FFNs (see section 2 for a formal treatment). This lets MoE LLMs match the latency and FLOPs of a much smaller dense model while approaching the quality of a much larger one. However, deploying MoEs for inference presents challenges. Efficient inference often relies on Speculative Decoding (SD) (Leviathan et al., 2022), which produces multiple tokens per step using a cheap approximation model to draft tokens that the target model verifies in parallel (see section 2 for details). Indeed, several of the newest frontier MoEs now ship native speculative decoding support built into the MoE architecture itself (NVIDIA, 2026; Xiaomi LLM-Core Team, 2026), underscoring the practical importance of the problem we tackle in this work. When performing inference with an MoE, the verification cost is no longer fixed. In this case, a parallel decoding call will take significantly longer than a single-token decoding call. This is because neighboring tokens may choose different experts, which means that a greater number of expert weights need to be transferred from dynamic random access memory (DRAM) to the Neural Processing Unit, or NPU (e.g. a TPU, GPU, or other accelerator) compared to single-token inference. Since decoding is bottlenecked by model weight transfers during inference, this extra parameter transfer will result in a significant decrease in inference throughput. This will reduce the computational savings normally provided by using sparsely-activated MoEs. In the worst case, all N experts could be transferred to the NPU. Concurrent work has begun to characterize this interaction between SD and MoE routing from a serving-time perspective (Saxena et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025); in this work, we instead ask whether the MoE itself can be trained to make SD verification cheaper. Our goal is to improve expert coactivation – e.g. to encourage neighboring tokens to choose similar sets of experts during verification to recover the performance benefits of SD. Rather than intervening at serving time, we achieve this entirely by changing how the MoE is trained : our method is simple, and applies four changes to the standard MoE training setup, leaving the SD algorithm and the deployed drafting mechanism untouched. First, we replace the standard local load-balancing loss with a global one (Qiu et al., 2025). Second, we use shared experts (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025). Third, we add a novel consistency loss to encourage neighboring token’s router logits to be similar. Finally, we add a novel autoregressive expert selection mechanism to ensure expert selections are similar for neighboring tokens. Figure 1 shows an overview of our approach. Figure 1a shows a standard MoE router verifying four tokens, requiring nine unique experts to be transferred from DRAM to the NPU; Figure 1b shows the same verification under our coactivation techniques, reducing this to five experts. Figure 1c shows that our method improves throughput across all SD window sizes γ. Figure 1d shows results for an MoE with 1 billion total and 353 million active parameters, compared to a 352 million parameter dense model: our method retains the accuracy benefits of MoEs ( 9% relative improvement over dense) while achieving 21% higher throughput than standard MoEs. Our contributions are as follows: • We analyze the performance of MoEs with SD and show that expert transfers decrease throughput. • We show that improving expert coactivation decreases memory transfer costs and improves SD’s efficacy for MoEs. • We propose a combination of training-time techniques – a global load-balancing loss, shared experts, a novel consistency loss, and a novel autoregressive expert selection – that improve expert coactivation during inference with SD, thereby improving overall throughput. 22 BACKGROUND

Speculative Decoding (SD) is a general technique for improving inference speed. It uses a smaller 

approximation model alongside the deployed target model : the approximation model autoregressively produces γ candidate tokens, and the target model verifies all γ + 1 tokens (the input plus the 

γ candidates) in a single parallel call, accepting or rejecting each candidate such that the output distribution exactly matches autoregressive decoding with the target model alone. We refer readers to Leviathan et al. (2022) for the full verification procedure. The key insight leveraged by SD is that model inference for modest context lengths is bottlenecked by memory bandwidth between DRAM and the NPU. For this reason, a parallel decoding step with a modest window size (for instance, 8) takes approximately the same amount of time as a decoding step with a window size of 1, since the same number of model weights are transferred to the NPU in both cases. By speculating future tokens with a computationally inexpensive approximation model, SD allows a target model to produce multiple tokens per inference call, resulting in higher throughput. The improvement in throughput depends on the acceptance rate, or the probability of accepting tokens produced by the approximation model. The overall throughput can be approximated as 

s = v(γ, A, M)

A(1) γ + M(γ + 1) , (2) where v(·) is the (average) number of tokens produced in an SD step, A/M are the approxima-tion/target models, and A(x)/M(x) are their runtimes on x input tokens. Note that v(·) ≤ γ + 1 ,since at most γ + 1 tokens can be produced in one step. Throughput is measured in tokens generated per second.

## 4 IMPROVING EXPERT COACTIVATION TO IMPROVE SPEED 

To improve the runtime of MoEs with SD, we can reduce the number of experts activated by the set of tokens in the SD window. We hypothesize that we can encourage neighboring tokens to work together to choose overlapping experts with a minimal impact on accuracy. We accomplish this with four training-time changes to the MoE router: a global load-balancing loss (subsection 4.1), shared experts (subsection 4.2), a novel consistency loss (subsection 4.3), and a novel autoregressive expert selection (subsection 4.4). Finally, we introduce a novel autoregressive expert selection algorithm to replace the standard per-token TopK selection in the router R, under which neighboring tokens select experts independently. Within each window of γ + 1 tokens, we autoregressively choose experts for each token so that neighboring tokens’ selections become correlated: each subsequent token retains at least k − l experts chosen by the previous token, then fills the remaining selections with its own highest-ranked experts. ## 6 RELATED WORK 

MoEs scale model capacity while controlling inference cost by activating a sparse subset of expert FFNs per token (Jacobs et al., 1991; Shazeer et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2022). Krajewski et al. (2024) and Ludziejewski et al. (2025) derive MoE scaling laws showing the compute-optimal expert count stays low until model scale exceeds several billion parameters. Our setting differs: we assume enough memory for all experts, with DRAM-to-NPU bandwidth as the binding constraint, not compute. Local vs. global load-balancing losses also affect expert specialization (Qiu et al., 2025), which we build on in subsection 4.1. Skliar et al. (2024) explore post-training cache-reuse techniques for loading experts from flash on mobile devices; we instead target the number of distinct experts requested per verification step. Instruction-conditioned pruning (Hou et al., 2025) offers an MoE-free route to sparsity, without retaining a single model across tasks. Speculative Decoding (Leviathan et al., 2022) was originally analyzed for dense models, where verification is free regardless of window size. Two recent studies show how this breaks down for MoEs: Saxena et al. (2025) show speculation can inflate expert-weight transfers by 2-3×, sometimes causing a net slowdown, and propose an adaptive policy; Huang et al. (2025) instead find MoEs can benefit more from SD under the right batch-size regime. Other systems work reduces verification-time expert loads: training-free per-layer expert budgeting (McDanel et al., 2026), cost-aware draft selection that avoids “expert scattering” (Xie et al., 2026), and commitment-weighted, self-sizing verifier expert sets (Liang et al., 2026). All of these act at inference time on a fixed, trained MoE; our approach instead modifies router training, and is complementary to all of them. Closest to our approach, Suram (2026) train small ( ≤340M-parameter) MoE routers for cache locality via auxiliary losses, but report a negative result: cache-miss reductions violate a pre-registered perplexity gate. Wu et al. (2026) instead adapt the MoE backbone with an auxiliary cache-prediction module, improving cache hit rates without retraining router selection. We differ in objective and scale: we target coactivation within an SD verification window rather than cache locality generally, evaluating at the billion-parameter scale, above where Suram (2026) observe the tradeoff. A separate line of work trains the MoE itself to draft for itself, e.g. via self-distillation of a per-layer draft expert (Han, 2026) or self-speculative decoding on-device (Huang et al., 2026). These methods modify SD’s drafting side, whereas ours modifies only the target model’s router, and could be combined with any of them. 

## 7 CONCLUSION 

We investigate the performance of MoEs with SD. We find that SD demonstrates reduced efficacy on MoEs due to increased DRAM-to-NPU bandwidth usage during verification. We show that applying global load-balancing, shared experts, autoregressive expert selection, and consistency loss during MoE training encourages expert coactivation, improving throughput on downstream tasks without harming accuracy. ## B HYPERPARAMETER SETTINGS 

We train all models (dense and MoE) with the following settings. 

Training. Batch size 512 and sequence length 4096 . All models are trained on NVIDIA H100 GPUs. 

Optimization. We use the AdaStar optimizer (EMA = 0 .95 ) with a learning rate of 0.01 , linear warmup for 5000 steps followed by cosine decay ( α = 0 .005 ), and weight decay 3.16 × 10 −4.Gradients are rescaled to norm 1 if their norm exceeds 1, and updates are skipped entirely if the norm exceeds 100 .

Architecture. Vocabulary size 49 ,152 , 16 attention heads, and 8 KV heads. 

Loss functions. The dense model loss is cross-entropy plus a z-loss (scale 10 −6). The MoE loss additionally includes a load-balancing loss (weight 0.01 ) and a router z-loss (weight 10 −4). 13
