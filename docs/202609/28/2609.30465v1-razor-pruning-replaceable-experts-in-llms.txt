## ABSTRACT 

Mixture-of-experts (MoE) models activate few experts per token but store the full expert pool. Expert pruning reduces this storage burden; at a fixed pruning budget, the goal is to preserve the original model’s output distribution as closely as possible. Yet an expert’s usage or contribution magnitude does not by itself determine the damage caused by its removal. What matters is whether the surviving computation can replace its function. We introduce R AZOR , a training-free expert pruning method that scores functional replaceability using consensus residuals: deviations of expert outputs from the original weighted mixture. An exact single-deletion identity at a fixed layer input accounts for survivor renormalization and router-selected refill, providing local scores aggregated over calibration tokens for budgeted pruning without gradients or recovery training. On GLM-4.7-Flash, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731, and Hy3 at 25% and 50% expert removal, R AZOR achieves the highest nine-task macro average among the evaluated pruning methods in all eight settings. On the two backbones with matched REAP benchmark runs, it exceeds REAP by 2.12–5.59 points and wins all 36 paired task comparisons. It also lowers reverse KL relative to REAP in all four matched GLM-4.7-Flash and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B model–budget settings. Analysis of re-sponses generated by Qwen3.6-35B-A3B nevertheless reveals changes in diversity, formatting, and termination, underscoring that task retention and predictive fidelity do not ensure generation stability. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Mixture-of-experts (MoE) models increase parameter capacity while activating only a small subset of experts per token, but sparse computation does not remove the cost of storing the full expert pool (Shazeer et al., 2017; Lepikhin et al., 2021; Fedus et al., 2022). Whole-expert pruning addresses this memory burden by reducing the pool itself (Lu et al., 2024; Muzio et al., 2024; Lasby et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2024). At a fixed pruning budget, the objective is to preserve the original model’s output distribution as closely as possible. The central question is therefore not simply which experts contribute least, but which can be removed with the least disruption to the computation that remains. This is a question of functional replaceability .Common pruning scores characterize experts by how often they are selected or how large their outputs are (Muzio et al., 2024; Jaiswal et al., 2025; Lasby et al., 2026). These statistics describe usage and contribution size, but do not capture how expert outputs combine. Even weighting an output norm by its routing mass leaves this geometric information unspecified. A large contribution may be replaceable by the surviving mixture, whereas a small one may supply a component that the survivors do not recover. Importance measured in isolation is thus different from the damage caused by deletion: the latter depends on the computation available after removal. Two features of routed mixtures make this distinction consequential. Removing an expert renormalizes the survivors’ weights; router refill can additionally promote an unselected expert. More generally, even identical gates and output norms can correspond to different least-harmful deletions (Proposition 1). We introduce R AZOR to score experts by this functional replaceability. Its reference is the original weighted MoE output, which we call the consensus without assuming agreement among experts. Subtracting this reference exposes each expert’s deviation from the mixture to be preserved. These consensus residuals yield an exact expression for single-deletion output change at a fixed layer input, accounting jointly for survivor renormalization and router-selected refill; deletion without refill is a special case. R AZOR aggregates the resulting local scores over calibration tokens and retains the highest-scoring experts within each layer’s budget. The ranking uses only forward computation, without gradients, subset search, or recovery training, and captures expert-output geometry rather than routing transitions alone (Goel et al., 2026). Local output change is a surrogate for the distributional objective, not a guarantee: joint deletions interact and altered activations propagate across layers. The experiments test both the utility of this criterion and the limits of what it preserves. On GLM-4.7-Flash, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731, and Hy3 at 25% and 50% expert removal, R AZOR has the highest nine-task macro average among the evaluated pruning methods in all eight settings, exceeding REAP by 2.12–5.59 points on the two backbones where REAP was also benchmarked. These gains do not make aggressive pruning lossless: all four backbones lose macro performance relative to their originals at 50% removal. On matched GLM-4.7-Flash and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B checkpoints, R AZOR also lowers reverse KL relative to REAP in all four model–budget settings. Free-generation analyses on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B show why these two forms of retention must still be distinguished from generation stability. Diversity does not follow the task ranking; at 50% removal, R AZOR produces fewer stray closing delimiters than REAP but more length-limited finishes than the unpruned model. Functional replaceability therefore offers a useful pruning criterion, while task performance, predictive fidelity, and generation behavior remain distinct outcomes to evaluate.

## 3 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate four research questions. 

RQ1. How well does pruning preserve the output distribution? We compare predictive drift on shared reference prefixes across domains and routing conditions. 

RQ2. How well does pruning preserve downstream performance? We evaluate task utility across four backbones at two removal budgets. 

RQ3. How does pruning affect generation behavior? We examine response diversity, form, and termination separately from accuracy. 

RQ4. Which components of the scoring rule drive pruning quality, and what do they change in expert selection? We separate component contrasts from diagnostics of retained sets and interactions among deleted experts. 3.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

Obs 1. R AZOR reduces predictive drift, but KL and likelihood need not agree. Across the four matched GLM-4.7-Flash and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B settings, R AZOR has lower reverse KL than REAP in all four and than RCS-LOO in three (Table 6). A separate routing-stratified comparison across all four backbones illustrates the distinction between fidelity measures: at 50% removal, RCS-LOO lowers KL relative to REAP in 72 of 80 model–domain-group–decile estimates, but keeps PPL closer to the original in only 47 (Appendix E.4). Reduced distributional drift therefore need not imply better preservation of reference-token likelihood. 

Obs 2. Gains cover most domains, but do not transfer uniformly. RAZOR lowers KL relative to REAP in 5/6/6/6 of eight domains at 25% removal and 7/5/8/7 at 50%, in GLM-4.7-Flash, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731, and Hy3 order. Yet Qwen3.6-35B-A3B’s equal-domain held-out mean at 50% is 0.2% higher than REAP’s, and the relative-change interval includes zero. Aggregate gains thus coexist with domain-level regressions. ## 4 RELATED WORK 

Expert scoring. Whole-expert pruning often ranks experts by routing frequency or activation norms (Muzio et al., 2024; Jaiswal et al., 2025). REAP analyzes substitution and survivor renormalization, but ranks experts by mean routing-weighted output norms conditional on use (Lasby et al., 2026). Our focus is instead functional replaceability: consensus residuals measure an expert’s output relative to the routed mixture, and the deletion counterfactual determines how survivor renormalization and router refill affect that mixture. The distinction is the quantity ranked, not the observation that deletion changes routing. 

Candidate-set search. Lu et al. (2024) cache layer inputs and outputs, enumerate retained expert combinations, and select the subset minimizing reconstruction error. EEP uses evolutionary search over router-mapping and expert-merging matrices, separating discrete pruning from continuous merging (Liu et al., 2024). These approaches evaluate compressed configurations rather than relying only on independent expert rankings. Our local deletion identities avoid subset search but do not solve joint selection: individually replaceable experts need not be jointly replaceable, and a refill candidate may itself be pruned. 

Expert relations. STUN uses router-derived behavioral similarity for clustering, followed by representative selection or selective reconstruction and then unstructured pruning (Lee et al., 2025). SHAPE constructs coalition-aware scores from routing co-occurrence and a coalition-utility proxy (Zhang, 2026). MAESTRO derives importance from cross-layer routing transitions but selects experts within each layer; its main results include attention-only LoRA recovery (Goel et al., 2026). These methods differ in both their relational signals and their compression procedures. R AZOR instead measures explicit output geometry under fixed-input deletion counterfactuals, without recovery train-ing. Neither relational scoring nor local exactness alone implies optimal joint selection. Appendix F details these connections and complementary approaches based on merging, router adaptation, budget allocation, and finer-grained compression. 9Work in Progress 

## 5 CONCLUSION 

We presented R AZOR , a training-free expert pruning method built on functional replaceability rather than contribution magnitude alone. Consensus residuals measure each expert relative to the original routed mixture, providing local deletion scores that account for survivor renormalization and router-selected refill. Across four post-trained MoE models at 25% and 50% expert removal, RAZOR achieves the highest nine-task macro average among the evaluated pruning methods in all eight settings. It also lowers reverse KL relative to REAP in all four matched GLM-4.7-Flash and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B fidelity comparisons. Analyses of generated responses reveal a complementary challenge: preserving task performance and predictive fidelity does not ensure stable generation behavior. Together, these results support pruning according to what the remaining computation can replace, while evaluating compression through both retained capability and changes in how the model generates. 

## 6 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

RAZOR scores each single-expert deletion using one router-selected refill candidate from the original expert pool. That candidate may itself be removed when multiple experts are pruned, so the replace-ment assumed during scoring need not remain available in the deployed model. A natural extension is to condition refill estimates on the retained expert set, or to model candidate survival while that set is still being selected. Considering deeper candidates in the router’s selection ranking and updating refill estimates as selection proceeds could better approximate the replacements available after pruning. Such approaches may improve expert selection, at the cost of additional expert-output computation and selection iterations. Candidate availability is only part of the joint-pruning problem: interacting deletions and changes to later-layer inputs also lie beyond the current fixed-input, single-deletion criterion. Our experiments cover four backbones and two removal budgets, but individual comparisons and diagnostics have narrower scope. Downstream benchmarks against Frequency, EAN, and REAP cover only GLM-4.7-Flash and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, and R AZOR ’s free-generation results are limited to Qwen3.6-35B-A3B (Table 5). Unconfirmed hash-layer matching prevents attributing DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731’s full benchmark gap to learned-router saliency. Calibration comparisons also have incomplete evaluation-input matching in some settings. These coverage and protocol limits are detailed in Appendices B.5 and D. Broader matched evaluations would help establish how well the observed gains transfer across routing designs and calibration distributions. Physical pruning reduces stored parameters, but retaining the same top-k does not imply a pro-portional reduction in active expert computation. The current scorer also reconstructs local expert outputs (Appendix A.4); scoring runtime, serving latency, energy use, peak memory, and end-to-end acceleration have not been measured. Evaluating these costs alongside retained capability and gener-ation behavior would clarify the practical trade-off between richer refill estimates and compression efficiency.

## ETHICS STATEMENT 

This work studies expert pruning of post-trained MoE language models, aiming to reduce model storage requirements while retaining their capabilities. Removing experts can also alter behaviors shaped by post-training, including safety-related responses, even when aggregate task performance is preserved. Agreement with the original model on safety-related inputs measures predictive fidelity; it does not establish safety or preservation of alignment. We therefore emphasize independent safety and alignment evaluation before deployment, particularly in safety-sensitive applications. The study uses previously released calibration data (Appendix B.1) and involves no human-subject research. 10 Work in Progress

Output chunking does not address the memory occupied by model weights. For models that exceed the available device capacity, the implementation also supports a layer-wise schedule: load one decoder layer, propagate every sequence in the current calibration shard through it, collect its statistics, and release its weights before loading the next layer. The shard’s hidden states and model-specific cross-layer state remain resident. Dividing calibration into smaller disjoint shards therefore controls a different memory cost from token chunking. For DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731, attention and indexer queries are also chunked, avoiding full query-by-key score workspaces while retaining their key coverage. These schedules support the same score definition; their availability does not establish which schedule was used by every reported checkpoint. Calibration examples are tokenized and packed before collection. Streaming here concerns the online reduction of statistics and staged loading of weights, rather than the sampling of calibration examples. A.4.3 ARCHITECTURE -SPECIFIC ROUTING 

The derivation requires normalized selected weights, not a particular router parameterization. We preserve each architecture’s native selection rule and score function: softmax probabilities for Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, sigmoid scores for GLM-4.7-Flash, DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731’s native score transformation, and sigmoid scores for Hy3. Where a correction bias determines selection, it is not added to the mixture weights. Selected unmodified scores are renormalized before evaluating the damage formula. The implementation retains the routed-output scale λ; omitting this common positive factor from the displayed score leaves within-layer rankings unchanged. DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 includes three fixed-hash layers whose expert identities come from a frozen token-to-expert table. They are handled separately from learned-router saliency. To retain 

B experts, we count occurrences over the entire table and select the most frequent experts; these are table counts, not frequencies weighted by calibration-token occurrence. Each table row first preserves its surviving expert identities. Removed entries are then replaced, in slot order, by the cosine-nearest surviving router-weight vector not already used in that row. With B ≥ k, the result has k distinct routes. Reindexing uses the same survivor order as parameter slicing. This is a routing repair, not expert-weight merging or an optimal assignment. The routing-stratified study shares this hash-layer selection across criteria; matching remains unconfirmed for all downstream checkpoints (Appendix B.5). 17 Work in Progress A.4.4 PHYSICAL PRUNING 

We slice expert-indexed tensors and corresponding router rows with the same keep indices, leaving dense blocks and shared experts unchanged. Under a common retained width, the result is a smaller MoE checkpoint requiring neither runtime masks nor custom sparse kernels. A separate option retains hash layers at full width; this mixed-width model requires a compatible architecture configuration and loading implementation. For Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, reducing 256 to 128 routed experts per layer yields an approximately 19B-parameter model. Removing experts reduces stored parameters, but preserving top-k does not proportionally reduce active expert computation. We do not measure serving latency, energy use, or end-to-end acceleration.

Metric definitions. Metrics are computed per question and then averaged over questions. We adapt the distinct-n approach of Li et al. (2016) to model tokens, using n ∈ { 2, 4} in the archived analysis and n ∈ { 2, 3, 4} in Figure 3. Distinct-n divides the number of unique token n-grams pooled across 16 responses by their total occurrences, without crossing response boundaries. Jaccard distance averages 1 − | Gi ∩ Gj |/|Gi ∪ Gj | over all 120 response pairs, where Gi and Gj are response-level token 4-gram sets. Both lexical measures use each model family’s own tokenizer. Embedding distance averages pairwise cosine distances using the fixed all-MiniLM-L6-v2 encoder (Sentence Transformers, 2021). Our full-response extension splits text into non-overlapping chunks of at most 254 WordPieces, mean-pools token representations, and normalizes each chunk vector. We then normalize their length-weighted mean. This retains content beyond the first chunk but not chunk order; distances do not measure execution behavior or mathematical-strategy equivalence.

They exclude new-response and checkpoint-level variation.

## F EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

MoE compression methods differ in their removal unit, importance signal, and selection or adaptation procedure. These distinctions separate the local scoring question studied here from broader changes to the expert pool and router. F.1 WHOLE -EXPERT SALIENCY AND CONDITIONAL AGGREGATION 

Routing statistics offer inexpensive expert rankings. SEER-MoE uses activation counts or soft counts from routing probabilities, with either layerwise or global removal (Muzio et al., 2024). The study of Jaiswal et al. (2025) compares several expert-dropping criteria, including expert activation norms. Such statistics describe expert use or activation size without explicitly reconstructing the mixture after deletion. REAP is the closest magnitude-based comparator in our experiments. It averages wi∥fi∥2 over tokens on which expert i is selected, separating conditional contribution size from activation frequency (Lasby et al., 2026). Its pruning analysis considers substitution by a promoted expert and renormalization of surviving gates; its implemented saliency nevertheless uses the removed expert’s weighted output norm. Our distinction is therefore the quantity being ranked, not the use of conditional averaging or the observation that deletion can trigger refill. Consensus residuals retain the removed expert’s direction relative to the mixture, and RCS-Refill also includes the promoted expert’s weighted residual. Token-level scoring and cross-token aggregation remain separate choices. R AZOR uses conditional RMS rather than REAP’s conditional mean, while the component comparisons hold the token quantity fixed when varying aggregation (Appendix C). Neither conditioning nor RMS follows from the local deletion identity, and no aggregation rule is uniformly preferred by the available results. 38 Work in Progress F.2 EXPERT RELATIONS AND CANDIDATE -SET SELECTION 

Relational information is not unique to consensus-residual scoring. STUN clusters experts using router-weight similarity and co-activation, then retains representative experts or selectively recon-structs them from cluster parameter means before unstructured pruning (Lee et al., 2025). SHAPE constructs coalition-aware scores from routing co-occurrence, using a proxy for coalition utility rather than evaluating every coalition’s model loss (Zhang, 2026). MAESTRO builds cross-layer routing transitions and derives importance from their stationary distribution, but selects experts for removal independently within each layer (Goel et al., 2026). Its main results follow attention-only LoRA recovery with experts and routers frozen. These methods use different observations of expert relationships: router-derived behavioral similarity, co-activation, and routing connectivity. Our scores instead evaluate output change under specified deletion counterfactuals at a fixed layer input. The reference is the routed mixture, not a cluster representative or a routing graph. This distinction concerns the local score, not a claim that independent expert ranking captures all interac-tions. Residuals can reinforce or cancel under joint removal (Appendix A.3), so exact single-deletion quantities do not yield an exact set-selection algorithm. Other approaches evaluate candidate sets more directly. Lu et al. (2024) select retained expert combinations using layer-output reconstruction error. EEP uses evolutionary search to optimize expert pruning and merging configurations (Liu et al., 2024). These procedures search over compressed configurations rather than relying only on a one-shot scalar ranking. We retain the highest-scoring experts under a fixed layerwise budget; our local identities do not eliminate the combinatorial selection problem or establish superiority to subset search. F.3 MERGING , ROUTER ADAPTATION , AND BUDGET ALLOCATION 

Expert merging changes the surviving functions, rather than only selecting which original experts remain. MC-SMoE groups experts around high-usage representatives using router-logit similarity, aligns neurons, and averages expert parameters before low-rank and structured compression (Li et al., 2024). REAM protects high-saliency experts as centroids and merges similar non-centroid experts into them (Jha et al., 2026). Neither operation is the router refill modeled by RCS-Refill, which promotes an existing unselected expert without averaging its parameters with the removed expert. Router KD adapts only router parameters after compression, distilling the uncompressed model’s next-token distribution while freezing the experts and other backbone parameters (Hyeon & Do, 2026). It is a recovery procedure, not an expert-ranking criterion. Budget allocation is another distinct choice. GRAPE compares residual redundancy across layers to allocate non-uniform budgets, merging similar expert pairs within the selected layer (Zhang et al., 2026); MC-SMoE also permits adaptive layerwise merging ratios (Li et al., 2024). These examples show that merging, adaptation, and budget allocation are overlapping design choices rather than mutually exclusive method families. Our experiments use uniform layerwise removal ratios, leave retained expert weights unchanged, and perform no recovery training. Combining residual-based scores with merging, router adaptation, or non-uniform allocation is outside the present comparison; their compatibility and joint benefit are not established by our results. F.4 FINER -GRAINED COMPRESSION AND COMPARISON SCOPE 

MoE-Pruner sparsifies weights within experts using weight magnitudes and router-weighted input activation statistics (Xie et al., 2024). HEAPr treats feed-forward intermediate neurons as atomic expert units and uses output-space second-order information to estimate their importance (Li et al., 2026). Both operate at a finer granularity than removing entire routed experts. An atomic expert in this terminology is not one of the router’s original selectable experts. These approaches share an interest in preserving model behavior, but their removal units and compres-sion procedures differ from ours. We therefore discuss them as related approaches rather than treat their reported sparsity levels as matched whole-expert budgets. Within our setting, RCS, RCS-LOO, and RCS-Refill isolate alternative consensus-residual scoring rules; R AZOR denotes RCS-Refill with conditional RMS. Their empirical comparison concerns that fixed pruning protocol, not a universal ranking of MoE compression strategies. 39
