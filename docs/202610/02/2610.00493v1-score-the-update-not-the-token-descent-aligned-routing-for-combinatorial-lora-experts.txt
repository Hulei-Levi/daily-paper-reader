Abstract 

Mixture-of-LoRA-experts methods raise the ca-pacity of low-rank adaptation by routing each token to a few low-rank experts. Nearly all of them tie one input-side factor to one output-side factor per expert, and nearly all of them route by scoring the token: the router picks experts without seeing what any of them would write. We argue that the router should score the update. To first order, adding an expert’s update to a layer output lowers the loss by the inner product between that update and the negative loss gradi-ent at the output. This usefulness is quadratic in the token, so a router that is linear in the token sees only the part of it that runs through the token mean, and routers that rank experts by the norm of their own activations never see the output factor. If each expert is split into a reader (down-projection) and a writer (up-projection), the usefulness of every reader–writer pair be-comes an inner product in the shared rank-r

space, and all NANB pairs can be scored from 

NA +NB vectors. We build V ANE on this iden-tity. A low-rank compass predicts the descent direction of each token. VANE scores every pair by the alignment between its update and the compass without forming any update, ac-tivates the top-k pairs with additive gates, and gives every pair its exact first-order router gra-dient. On single-domain commonsense reason-ing and a four-domain multi-task mixture with Llama-3.2-3B and Llama-3.1-8B, V ANE attains the best average among twelve PEFT and MoE-LoRA baselines, by 0.9–1.1 and 1.3–1.5 points respectively, with less than half the trainable pa-rameters of an 8-expert MoE-LoRA. Its router scores also track the measured usefulness of experts far more closely than token routers do. 

1 Introduction 

Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) fine-tunes a frozen language model by adding a trainable product BA 

to each weight matrix (Hu et al., 2022; Houlsby et al., 2019). When the training data mixes domains whose updates conflict, the standard remedy is a mixture of low-rank experts: a router sends each token to a few experts, so capacity grows while per-token cost stays small (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022; Dou et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024a; Wu et al., 2024a; Tian et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2025). Follow-up work refines how experts are allocated across layers (Gao et al., 2024; Liao et al., 2025), how many are active (Zhuang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025; Saliencro et al., 2026a), how finely capac-ity is split (Zhao et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2025a; Zeng et al., 2025), and how experts share parame-ters (Wang et al., 2025a; Cao et al., 2026a). These designs share two conventions. Each ex-pert welds one input-side factor Ai to one output-side factor Bi, and the router scores the token : it maps the hidden state x to logits without seeing what any expert would compute. Combinatorial designs mix and match factors (Zhang et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2025), and self-routing lets experts select themselves by the size of their own activations (Lv et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2025a; Han et al., 2025; Hou et al., 2026). But no design states what a router should estimate, so routers are refined by heuristics such as entropy control, dynamic k, expert communication, and se-mantic priors (Li et al., 2025; Zhuang et al., 2026; Mu et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026). We start from that question. Adding an expert’s update v to a layer output y changes the loss, to first order, by −⟨ δ, v ⟩, where δ = −∇ yℓ is the de-scent direction at the output. The usefulness of an expert for a token is thus the alignment between the update it would write and the direction in which the loss wants the output to move. Two consequences follow (Figure 1). Usefulness is second order in the token. Under a linear-response model, a router that is linear in x sees only the part of usefulness that runs through the token mean, and nothing for cen-tered tokens (Theorem 1). 2 Related Work 

Mixtures of LoRA experts. LoRAMoE, MixLoRA, and MoLE route tokens among paired LoRA experts (Dou et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024a; Wu et al., 2024a). Later work allocates experts by depth (Gao et al., 2024; Liao et al., 2025), aligns their optimization with full fine-tuning (Fan et al., 2025), makes k dynamic (Li et al., 2025; Zhuang et al., 2026; Saliencro et al., 2026a,b), lets experts communicate (Mu et al., 2026), injects semantic priors (Shi et al., 2026), or uses heterogeneous and spectral experts (Cao et al., 2026b; Jiang et al., 2026; Saliencro et al., 2026c); others drop the router (Zha et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2025), and clean routing alone does not prevent interference inside an adapter (Chowdhury et al., 2026). These methods improve how the router decides; we specify what it should estimate. 

Shared and combinatorial factors. LoRA’s A

extracts features and B produces the output (Zhu et al., 2024). HydraLoRA shares one A across 

B heads (Tian et al., 2024), MALoRA shares a down-projection subspace (Wang et al., 2025a), MoSLoRA mixes the two subspaces (Wu et al., 2024b), and CoMoL keeps experts as core ma-trices between shared factors (Cao et al., 2026a). Mix-and-match designs build many experts from few pieces: rank-one experts (Zhang et al., 2024; 2Zhao et al., 2025), residual trees (Zeng et al., 2025), shards and vector banks (Wang et al., 2025b; Li et al., 2024b), and factorized or product-key expert tensors (Oldfield et al., 2024; Lample et al., 2019; He, 2024). V ANE uses the simplest such structure, readers times writers, and is the first to exploit it for exact scoring. 

Coupling routing and computation. Autonomy-of-Experts ranks experts by the norm of their own low-rank activations (Lv et al., 2025). FlyLoRA routes with a frozen sparse projection (Zou et al., 2025a), FURINA by the angle between input and adapter (Han et al., 2025), Self-Routing by a sub-space of the hidden state (Mohamud et al., 2026), and CoMoL and PASs-MoE in the adapters’ la-tent space (Cao et al., 2026a; Hou et al., 2026); all read the input side only. Default MoE densi-fies the router gradient with moving averages of expert outputs (Panda et al., 2025), and GRIN esti-mates gradients through discrete routing (Liu et al., 2024a). V ANE ’s scores involve the writers and a forecast of the loss gradient, and its dense gradient is exact. Appendix A extends this review.

5.3 Ablations 

Table 3 changes one component at a time. 

The score matters most. Keeping the reader– writer structure but routing with a linear router over the 32 pairs costs 1.4 multi-task points, back to the level of LD-MoLE. A latent-linear router loses 1.3, reader-norm self-routing 1.9, and a product router 2.0. All four are first-order or writer-blind (Theo-rem 1), and their scores correlate with usefulness at ρ ≤ 0.24 , against 0.61. Removing calibration costs 0.6, and untying the writer queries 0.5. 

Training and structure. The dense first-order gradient is worth 0.9 points and raises ρ from 0.43 to 0.61; letting score gradients reach the experts costs 0.3. Pair-level balancing fights specialization (−0.5), and no balancing costs 1.1. Paired experts with the same score lose 1.0 and a single shared reader 0.8, so structure and score are complemen-tary. Separable gates lose 1.7 as their rank collapses (Proposition 4), and softmax gates 0.7. Adding the compass to the output does not help ( −0.2): its job is to point, not to write.

5.5 Efficiency, Sparsity, and Scale 

VANE -S and V ANE lie on the accuracy–parameter frontier (Figure 4a). With 4 readers, going from 2 to 8 writers adds 1.9 points, while with 8 writ-ers, going from 1 to 4 readers adds 0.8 (Figure 4b). Sparsity helps: k=4 of 32 pairs is best, a dense core loses 1.5 points, and V ANE beats the token router at every k (Figure 4c). Gains over LoRA grow as models shrink, from +3 .5 on 8B to +4 .4

on Llama-3.2-1B, at 1.6–2.0× those of LD-MoLE (Figure 4d). Training takes 1.26 × LoRA’s time and 34% less than LD-MoLE, and unmerged decoding takes 27.2 vs. 36.2 ms per token, because V ANE 

runs dense products instead of the per-expert ker-nels that dominate dynamic-adapter latency (Kong et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026a) (Table 14). 

6 Conclusion 

A router should estimate how much each expert’s update would lower the loss: to first order, the alignment between the update and the descent di-rection at the layer output. This quantity is in-visible to token routers for centered tokens and to reader-side scores always, but once experts are split into readers and writers it can be computed exactly for every pair in the low-rank latent space. VANE turns this into a router made of the experts themselves plus a small compass, which scores ev-ery pair, learns from every pair’s exact first-order effect, and does not depend on how experts are parameterized. It outperforms twelve PEFT and MoE-LoRA baselines with half the parameters of an 8-expert mixture. The same principle applies to any mixture whose experts factor through a shared low-dimensional space, including sparse MoE pre-training and adapter merging. 

Limitations 

Theorem 1 rests on a linear-response model with Gaussian tokens; it describes what router classes can represent, not how they train. Our evidence that the analysis transfers is the score–usefulness corre-8lation measured on real runs. Usefulness is a first-order, per-layer quantity. It ignores interactions between layers and between pairs, and it is myopic when the loss is far from locally linear. The dense router gradient uses the first-order surrogate for un-selected pairs and the true gradient only for selected ones. Calibrating scores by update norm gives up some static optimality (Appendix E): when token means dominate, a well-trained token router cap-tures a comparable share of the first-order gain. VANE computes every reader and every writer for each token. This is cheap at our sizes but grows lin-early with NA and NB , and large pools would need a sparse writer path. Our experiments cover mod-els up to 8B parameters and four domains. Larger backbones, more domains, and generation-heavy tasks remain to be tested, as do adapter merging and continual learning, where interference between tasks is the main obstacle (Zou et al., 2025a, 2026). 

Ethics Statement 

VANE is a general fine-tuning method and inherits the risks of the models and data it adapts, including biases in instruction-tuning corpora and the lower cost of producing specialized models, which also lowers the cost of misuse. All datasets are public and used under their licenses. The medical bench-marks measure multiple-choice accuracy, and a model adapted with V ANE is not a medical device. No human subjects or personal data are involved. AI assistants were used for language editing and for checking LaTeX; the method, proofs, and ex-perimental design are the authors’ own, and every reference was checked against its primary record.

A Extended Related Work 

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning. Adapters in-sert small bottleneck modules into a frozen net-work (Houlsby et al., 2019). LoRA instead adds a low-rank product to each weight matrix and can be merged after training (Hu et al., 2022). LLM-Adapters standardized the commonsense and arith-metic protocols we build on (Hu et al., 2023). Later variants change the parameterization or the initial-ization. DoRA separates magnitude from direc-tion (Liu et al., 2024b). PiSSA initializes from the principal singular subspace of the pretrained weight (Meng et al., 2024), and LoRA-GA from the gradient at initialization (Wang et al., 2024). VB-LoRA composes all factors from a shared vector bank (Li et al., 2024b), and MoS routes shards from global pools into each factor (Wang et al., 2025b). Two findings about LoRA’s factors guide our de-sign. First, A extracts features and B produces the output, and training B matters more than train-ing A (Zhu et al., 2024); A can even stay a frozen sparse random projection (Zou et al., 2025a). Sec-ond, the A matrices of LoRAs trained on different data are similar while their B matrices differ (Tian et al., 2024). V ANE does not assume this asymme-try. It lets the router discover it, and the learned usage shows writers carrying most of the domain information (§5.4). 

Mixtures of LoRA experts. Sparse mixtures of experts scale capacity by routing each token to a few feed-forward experts (Shazeer et al., 2017; Lepikhin et al., 2021; Fedus et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2022; Dai et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2024). Their soft and fully differentiable variants avoid discrete top-k selection (Puigcerver et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025c). Adapter mixtures bring this idea to fine-tuning. • Paired experts: LoRAMoE uses them to pro-tect world knowledge (Dou et al., 2024), and MixLoRA, MoLE, and AdaMoLE route among them per token (Li et al., 2024a; Wu et al., 2024a; Liu and Luo, 2024). • Allocation and optimization: MoLA and HMoRA allocate experts by depth or hierar-chically (Gao et al., 2024; Liao et al., 2025), HILO also varies their rank (Cong et al., 2025), and GOAT aligns the optimization of an SVD-structured mixture with full fine-tuning (Fan et al., 2025). • Adaptive compute: DynMoLE and LD-MoLE adapt the number of active experts (Li et al., 2025; Zhuang et al., 2026). CARE admits ex-perts until a confidence mass is reached (Salien-cro et al., 2026a), and VI-MoLE allocates experts by certified value of information (Saliencro et al., 2026b). • Richer routing and heterogeneous experts: Talk-LoRA lets experts exchange information before routing (Mu et al., 2026), and SAMoRA aligns routing with input semantics (Shi et al., 2026). MoA mixes heterogeneous adapter types (Cao et al., 2026b), FourierMoE and FRAME move experts to spectral or fractional domains (Jiang et al., 2026; Saliencro et al., 2026c), and adaptive shared experts combine task-specific and shared experts (Yang et al., 2025b). • Other structures: TT-LoRA MoE trains ten-sorized experts separately and routes among them (Kunwar et al., 2025), MoEGen gen-erates instance-specific updates from expert codes (Zeng et al., 2026), and Localized LoRA-MoE routes over blocks of the weight ma-trix (Barazandeh et al., 2026). • Pruning and MoE backbones: DMEP prunes ex-perts per module once routing stabilizes (Li et al., 2026b), EPnG prunes and grows expert ranks un-der a fixed budget (Lee et al., 2026), and MoE 2-LoRA and NSFT adapt MoE language models with routed or sub-expert adapters (Yang et al., 2026a; Tan et al., 2026). Several works question routing itself. CD-LoRA and Align-LoRA remove routers and align repre-14 sentations in a shared space (Zha et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2025), and Chowdhury et al. (2026) show that disjoint routing does not prevent interference inside an adapter. All of these methods leave open what quantity a router should estimate. V ANE an-swers this question and derives its router from the answer. 

Shared factors, combinatorial experts, and ten-sor views. Sharing factors across experts saves parameters and couples experts. • Shared or mixed subspaces: HydraLoRA shares a single down-projection across heads (Tian et al., 2024), MALoRA shares a low-rank sub-space (Wang et al., 2025a), and MoSLoRA learns a mixer between the two LoRA subspaces (Wu et al., 2024b). • Core experts: CoMoL stores experts as core ma-trices between shared factors and routes in the core space (Cao et al., 2026a). Self-Routed Ten-sor Adapters blend slices of a shared Tucker core with weights computed from the low-rank pro-jection (Yadav, 2026). • Many experts from few parts: T-REX mixes and matches rank-one experts so that the expert sub-space grows quadratically with linear parameter cost (Zhang et al., 2024). SMoRA treats every rank as an expert (Zhao et al., 2025), S’MoRE assembles residual experts along trees (Zeng et al., 2025), and Dynamic Subspace Compo-sition builds updates from a sparse set of basis vectors (Khasia, 2025). • Factorized expert tensors: product keys retrieve from a Cartesian product of sub-keys (Lample et al., 2019), and PEER uses them to route among a million tiny experts (He, 2024). μMoE com-putes a mixture over a factorized expert ten-sor (Oldfield et al., 2024). VANE ’s reader–writer product is the simplest com-binatorial structure in this family. Its distinctive use is computational: because every pair factors through the same rank-r latent, the router can evalu-ate every pair’s first-order effect in that latent space. 

Routing signals. Token routers map the hidden state to logits, possibly with priors such as semantic clusters (Zhang et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2026) or the routing history of earlier layers (Qiu et al., 2026). Self-routing methods remove the router. • AoE ranks experts by the norms of their own low-rank activations (Lv et al., 2025). • FlyLoRA uses the top-k coordinates of a frozen sparse projection as an implicit router (Zou et al., 2025a). Its decorrelation properties also help continual learning (Zou et al., 2026, 2025b), and NeuroLoRA adds a context gate before selec-tion (Yang et al., 2026b). • FURINA activates experts by the angle between the input and each adapter’s direction (Han et al., 2025). Self-Routing uses a subspace of the hid-den state as logits (Mohamud et al., 2026), and geometric routing uses cosine similarity in a low-dimensional metric space (Ternovtsii and Bilak, 2026). • PASs-MoE calibrates routing with each expert’s pathway activations to prevent router–expert co-drift (Hou et al., 2026). In the terms of Theorem 1, every one of these scores is either affine in the token or a function of the input-side activations alone. None involves the writer or the loss. On the training side, Default MoE gives the router a dense gradient by substi-tuting moving averages of expert outputs for un-selected experts (Panda et al., 2025). GRIN esti-mates gradients through discrete routing (Liu et al., 2024a), and DAG-MoE learns how selected experts are aggregated (Feng et al., 2026). V ANE ’s dense gradient differs from Default MoE’s in being exact to first order, which the factorization makes afford-able, and it is the gradient of the quantity the router is designed to estimate. 

Value of information and adaptive compute. 

VI-MoLE also routes by an estimate of risk reduc-tion (Saliencro et al., 2026b). It learns the residual risk of each prefix of router-ordered experts, cer-tifies it on held-out data, and decides how much compute to spend and when to abstain. V ANE an-swers a complementary question, which pairs to activate at a fixed k. It does so with a first-order estimate that is exact during training and forecast at test time. The two could be combined: V ANE ’s scores could order the candidates whose value VI-MoLE certifies.

Initialization. Readers and V use LoRA’s Kaiming-uniform initialization, and U is drawn from N (0 , 1/d ′). Writers are drawn from

Inference. At test time the compass is the router. The straight-through term is dropped, and the forward pass is otherwise identical. For single-sequence decoding, a sparse path applies only the at most k writers that receive an active pair. For batched decoding the dense path is faster, and we report it. Dynamic adapters are slow to decode mainly because of fragmented per-expert kernel launches, not because of their arithmetic (Kong et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026a). V ANE ’s dense path is-sues the same few kernels for every token. Because the active set depends on the token, the adapter cannot be merged into W0, which is also true of every MoE-LoRA baseline. 

Memory. Per token, V ANE stores NAr + re +

NANB activations for the backward pass, which is 56 floats per adapted matrix at the default. Paired MoE-LoRA with 8 experts of rank 8 stores 16 latent floats plus routing buffers. 

G Experimental Setup 

Data. Single-domain. Commonsense170K is the union of the training sets of the eight commonsense benchmarks, as released with LLM-Adapters (Hu et al., 2023). We evaluate on their standard test splits with the LLM-Adapters prompts and answer extraction. 

Multi-task. We draw 30k examples from each of four sources with a fixed seed, giving 120k exam-ples: • MetaMathQA (Yu et al., 2024); • Magicoder-Evol-Instruct-110K (Wei et al., 2024); • the MedMCQA training split (Pal et al., 2022); • Commonsense170K. We evaluate on GSM8K (1,319 test problems), MATH (5,000), HumanEval (164), MBPP (500 test problems), the MedMCQA dev split (4,183), PubMedQA’s 500 labeled test questions, ARC-c (1,172), and the WinoGrande dev split (1,267). Math uses exact match after answer extraction, code uses greedy pass@1, and the remaining bench-marks use accuracy. 

Prompts and decoding. All multi-task data use one Alpaca-style instruction template. Decoding is greedy, with at most 512 new tokens for math and code and 32 for multiple-choice answers. 

Baselines. Table 6 lists every configuration. Pa-rameter counts are computed from the Llama layer shapes by code/param_count.py and appear as Param% in Tables 1 and 2. All methods adapt the q, k, v, o, gate, up, and down projections. Mix-tures use their auxiliary losses at the recommended weights. Every method, V ANE included, uses the same optimizer, schedule, batch size, number of epochs, and learning rate ( 2×10 −4). We tuned only the learning rate, over {10 −4, 2×10 −4, 3×10 −4}

on a held-out slice of the training mixture, and 

2×10 −4 was best or tied for every method. 

Hyperparameters and compute. Table 7 lists the training configuration. Runs use 8 NVIDIA H100 80GB GPUs with bfloat16 and FlashAtten-tion. A multi-task run on Llama-3.1-8B takes 3.9 hours for V ANE (Table 14). All numbers are means over seeds 0, 1, and 2.

AoE’s premise, that an expert knows best whether it should process a to-ken, is close in spirit to ours (Lv et al., 2025). Our analysis sharpens it. What an expert can know from its own activations is how strongly the token lies in its input subspace, which Theorem 1(ii) shows is writer-blind. By Proposition 3, this quantity also depends on an arbitrary gauge. What decides usefulness is whether the expert’s output points where the loss wants to go. FlyLoRA’s implicit router (Zou et al., 2025a) and CoMoL’s core-space router (Cao et al., 2026a) share the input-side view. All three designs gain parameter efficiency by rout-ing in a low-dimensional space, a property V ANE 

keeps. 

Additive versus normalized gates. Softmax nor-malization makes the per-token update a convex combination of expert updates, so its total size is fixed however many complementary directions a token needs. Additive sigmoid gates let each pair contribute at its own scale, which matches the first-order view (Proposition 4). Normalized sigmoid gating is also statistically favorable for expert estimation (Nguyen et al., 2025). When independently trained LoRAs are composed, soft weighting changes the unit-scale additive update under which each adapter was trained (Molavi et al., 2026). V ANE avoids the issue because its pairs are trained jointly with additive gates. 

The compass as a learned model of the loss. 

The compass is a rank-re linear model of E[δ | x],trained only through the routing signal. Proposi-tion 2 shows that each routing step moves it toward 

δ within the span of the candidate updates, and Figure 3b shows that it gets there. Two extensions are natural. A direct auxiliary loss could regress the compass onto the backpropagated δ, which is available in the same backward hook. A nonlinear compass, for example a small MLP, would lift the linear-response assumption of Theorem 1 at a small cost. We kept the linear compass because it is the object the theory analyzes and it already reaches the saturation point of the rank sweep. 

Beyond LoRA adapters. Nothing in Proposi-tion 1 is specific to fine-tuning. Any mixture whose experts factor through a shared low-dimensional space can score all experts by their first-order effect at the cost of a few latent vectors. Examples are low-rank or product-key experts in sparse MoE pre-training (Oldfield et al., 2024; He, 2024) and banks of task adapters to be composed at test time. For merging independently trained LoRAs, where in-terference between tasks is the main difficulty (Zou et al., 2025a), the same scores could choose which reader–writer combinations to keep. These uses require care because the gauge is shared only if the factors are trained jointly or the readers are frozen and common to all adapters. 

Failure modes. (i) Nonlinear regime. When the loss is far from linear in the output, for example on tokens with very confident wrong predictions, first-order usefulness misestimates the benefit of large updates. The additive gates are bounded by 1, which limits the damage. (ii) Compass collapse. 

If the compass norm shrinks, all scores approach zero and gates approach 0.5. Balancing keeps the router’s softmax informative, and we did not ob-serve collapse. (iii) Correlated writers. If two writers become nearly parallel, their pairs receive similar scores and the effective number of experts drops. Marginal balancing does not prevent this; a small orthogonality penalty between writers would. (iv) Very large pools. The dense path computes all readers and writers. Beyond a few hundred pairs, the sparse writer path or product-key retrieval over the score matrix becomes necessary. 

Practical recommendations. Invest capacity in writers, with NB ≈ 2NA, and keep k near NA so that active pairs can use distinct readers. Set re to about 2r. Keep marginal balancing on. Monitor the score–usefulness correlation with the provided backward hook. It is cheap, and a drop signals a stale or collapsed compass before accuracy de-grades. 

Broader impact. VANE makes multi-domain adaptation more accurate at a lower parameter cost and less training time than comparable mix-tures. This lowers the barrier to building special-ized models, with the usual dual-use implications. The method adds no data, capabilities, or deploy-ment channels beyond those of the underlying fine-tuning pipeline. 24
