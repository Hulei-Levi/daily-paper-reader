Abstract 

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models enable parameter scaling with limited per-token computation by activating only a small subset of experts for each token, but deploying them still requires loading the complete expert pool into memory. Structured expert pruning can effectively reduce the memory usage by removing experts. However, existing pruning methods either use expert ranking criteria that are not well aligned with model performance or rely on effective expert subset searching that is computationally expensive. Moreover, these methods typically overlook the routing-behavior redundancy among the retained experts. In this paper, we propose MoE Pruning via Router Bias Learning and Expert Approximation (MoRA), a framework for structured MoE expert pruning. We introduce a learnable router bias for each expert and optimize these biases by minimizing the language-modeling loss and a routing-diversity regularizer. The learned router biases sharpen the routing probability distributions to identify experts critical to model performance while encouraging the selection of experts with diverse routing preferences. In addition, we introduce an expert approximation mechanism as a post-pruning enhancement. It leverages the remaining experts to approximate the outputs of pruned experts by affine transformation, further improving the performance of the pruned model. We evaluate MoRA on Qwen3-30B-A3B, DeepSeek-V2-Lite, and Moonlight-16B-A3B, removing 25% and 50% of the routed experts in each MoE layer. Extensive experiments on nine zero-shot benchmarks show that MoRA outperforms state-of-the-art pruning algorithms. Our code will be released. 

# 1 Introduction 

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models activate only a small subset of experts for each token, enabling model scaling with limited per-token computation (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022). Although sparse activation reduces inference computation, deploying MoE models still requires loading the full expert pool, resulting in substantial memory usage. Structured expert pruning offers a direct way to reduce this cost by removing less important experts while retaining the sparse routing structure (Lee et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026). Hence, the core challenge is to determine which experts can be removed while preserving the predictive behavior of the model. Early expert pruning methods rank experts using aggregate routing statistics, such as activation frequency and average gate values (Koishekenov et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2024). However, these expert ranking criteria may not directly align with the model predictive performance. For example, frequency-based pruning may assign higher scores to experts that frequently process common function words with less semantic information

To address these issues, we propose MoE pruning via Router bias learning and expert Approximation (MoRA), a framework for structured MoE expert pruning. As shown in Figure 1 (c), we first introduce a learnable router bias for each expert. These experts are originally pretrained under the load-balancing constraints. The biases can sharpen routing probability distributions over experts and serve as scores for selecting experts. To learn effective biases, we optimize them with the language-modeling (LM) loss and a routing-diversity regularizer. The former encourages higher biases for experts critical to predictive performance, while the latter discourages experts with similar routing preferences from receiving high biases simultaneously. Herein, similar routing preference means that experts tend to receive similar routing weights for the same tokens. We also introduce an expert approximation mechanism in our MoRA as a post-pruning enhancement. Specifically, we assign a retained expert as a proxy for each pruned expert and apply an affine transformation to approximate the output of the pruned expert with the retained experts, thereby mitigating the performance degradation caused by pruning. We evaluate MoRA on Qwen3-30B-A3B Yang et al. (2025), DeepSeek-V2-Lite Liu et al. (2024), and Moonlight-16B-A3B Liu et al. (2025) under two deployment budgets that remove 25% and 50% of the routed experts in each MoE layer. We evaluate zero-shot downstream performance on nine benchmarks. Further analysis reveals that the association between the learned router biases and predictive performance is stronger in models with more experts and in deeper MoE layers. Our main contributions are as follows: • We propose MoRA, a structured MoE expert pruning framework that learns router biases using the LM loss and a routing-diversity regularizer. The learned biases serve as scores for selecting experts with high predictive importance and diverse routing preferences. • We propose expert approximation as a post-pruning enhancement, which uses lightweight affine transformations to approximate the outputs of pruned experts with the retained experts. It effectively mitigates the performance degradation caused by pruning. • We evaluate MoRA on Qwen3-30B-A3B, DeepSeek-V2-Lite, and Moonlight-16B-A3B at 25% and 250% routed-expert pruning. Results on nine zero-shot benchmarks show that MoRA outperforms other pruning baselines while better preserving the predictive performance. 

# 2 Related Work 

Sparse MoE models. Sparse MoE models increase model capacity while limiting per-token computation by activating only a subset of experts (Shazeer et al., 2017). Large-scale systems such as GShard (Lepikhin et al., 2020) and Switch Transformers (Fedus et al., 2022) established routing and training recipes for this regime. Despite sparse activation, storing all expert parameters remains memory-intensive. This motivates expert pruning to reduce memory requirements while preserving predictive performance. 

MoE Pruning. Weight-level pruning methods, such as SparseGPT, Wanda, and MoE-Pruner, can reduce MoE parameters by pruning individual weights within experts (Frantar and Alistarh, 2023; Sun et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024). However, these unstructured pruning methods directly reduce storage requirements and typically require specialized hardware support to achieve inference speedup. Expert-level pruning instead removes entire experts, directly reducing storage requirements. Early methods rank experts using activation frequency and gating scores (Koishekenov et al., 2023). NAEE (Lu et al., 2024) selects expert subsets that minimize the reconstruction loss between the original and pruned layer outputs. However, enumerating the subsets leads to expensive computation as the number of experts and the pruning budget increase. STUN (Lee et al., 2025) avoids exhaustive subset evaluation through expert clustering and parameter-space approximations. Complementary approaches compress MoE models through expert merging (Li et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025), or replacement with lightweight modules (Zhang et al., 2026; Hao et al., 2026). However, modifying or replacing expert functions can introduce additional approximation errors. 

# 3 MoRA 

In this section, we propose MoE pruning via Router bias learning and expert approximation (MoRA), a framework for structured MoE pruning. We introduce a learnable router bias for each expert to sharpen the routing probability distributions, and optimize these biases using the LM loss and a routing-diversity regularizer. The resulting biases serve as expert selection scores that balance predictive importance and routing diversity. Then, MoRA performs expert approximation based on affine transformation as a post-pruning enhance-ment. Specifically, MoRA estimates the directed proxy fidelity between retained and removed experts. It fits a channel-wise affine transformation from each candidate retained-expert output to the corresponding removed-expert output and evaluates the resulting approximation quality. For each removed expert, MoRA selects the retained expert with the highest proxy fidelity as its proxy. MoRA then reuses the fitted affine map to approximate the output of pruned expert during inference.

The key challenge of MoE pruning is to retain an expert set that preserves the performance of the original MoE model. A desirable retained set should satisfy two requirements. First, it should preserve as much of the next-token prediction capability as possible. Second, the retained experts should exhibit diverse routing preferences, allowing a limited number of retained experts to cover complementary routing behaviors. Unlike methods pruning based on statistics or reconstruction, we propose a learning-based expert pruning mechanism to efficiently identify experts that are critical to model performance. During pretraining, sparse MoE models commonly regulate expert traffic through auxiliary load-balancing objectives (Yang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024) or expert-level correction biases (Liu et al., 2025) to mitigate routing collapse and encourage more balanced expert utilization. Building on this, we introduce an additional learnable router bias for each expert to sharpen routing probability distributions. These biases are optimized with the LM loss and a routing-diversity regularizer. The learned biases therefore can serve as retention scores that balance predictive performance and routing diversity.

No additional optimization is required after proxy assignment. The exported model stores only the retained experts, the target-proxy mappings, and the fitted channel-wise affine parameters. The removed expert parameters and proxy fidelity scores are discarded. # 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Models and pruning budgets. We evaluate Qwen3-30B-A3B (Yang et al., 2025), DeepSeek-V2-Lite (Liu et al., 2024), and Moonlight-16B-A3B (Liu et al., 2025). Qwen3 has 48 MoE layers with 128 routed experts per layer and activates eight experts per token. DeepSeek-V2-Lite and Moonlight each have 26 MoE layers with 64 routed experts, two shared experts, and six activated routed experts per token. We remove either 25% or 50% of the routed experts in every MoE layer, retaining 96/64 experts for Qwen3 and 48/32 experts for the 6other two models. Shared experts and all non-expert parameters are preserved, and the original routing top-k

is unchanged. 

Baselines. Activation Count ranks routed experts according to how often they are selected by the pretrained router on the calibration corpus and retains the highest-ranked experts in each layer. Average Gate Mass 

ranks experts by their normalized top-k routing weights averaged over all calibration tokens, incorporating both selection frequency and routing confidence (Koishekenov et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2024). MoNE combines expert access frequency with output fluctuation to identify important experts and replaces less important experts with lightweight approximations (Zhang et al., 2026). MC-SMoE groups experts according to the similarity of their router-logit patterns and constructs representative experts through activation-frequency-weighted merging (Li et al., 2024). HC-SMoE hierarchically clusters experts using their output features and merges the experts within each cluster (Chen et al., 2025). 

Implementation Details. Following the common practice in existing MoE pruning methods (Ling et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2024), we conduct experiments using C4 (Raffel et al., 2020) as the calibration dataset. The router biases are learned on C4 with 1000 iterations for Qwen3-30B-A3B, DeepSeek-V2-Lite and Moonlight-16B-A3B. For post-pruning enhancement, we divide the sampled C4 calibration corpus into two non-overlapping subsets of equal size: Each subset contains 64 sequences of 1024 tokens. We set the hyperparameters λd = 0 .8 and qmin = 0 .4.

Evaluation benchmarks. Following the previous MoE pruning methods (Zhang et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2024; Ling et al., 2024; An et al., 2024), we use lm-evaluation-harness (Gao et al., 2021) for zero-shot evaluation on BoolQ (Clark et al., 2019), RTE (Wang et al., 2019), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2021), PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020), OpenBookQA (Mihaylov et al., 2018), ARC-Easy (Clark et al., 2018), ARC-Challenge (Clark et al., 2018), and MMLU. HellaSwag, PIQA, OpenBookQA, ARC-Easy, and ARC-Challenge use acc_norm ; the other tasks use acc . Avg. is the mean of these nine percentages. 

4.2 Main Results 

Comparison with baselines. Tables 1, 2, and 3 compare MoRA with five pruning baselines on nine zero-shot benchmarks. MoRA achieves the highest average score across all three architectures at both 25% and 50% pruning ratios. Compared with the strongest baseline in each setting, MoRA improves the average score by 1.14, 0.43, and 1.01 percentage points at 25% pruning, and by 6.43, 0.56, and 0.97 points at 50% pruning, on Qwen3-30B-A3B, DeepSeek-V2-Lite, and Moonlight-16B-A3B, respectively. We attribute the weaker performance of HC-SMoE on Qwen3 to a potential mismatch between its clustering criterion, based on average output similarity, and the high degree of expert specialization in Qwen3. Merging experts under this aggregate criterion may reduce the diversity of expert outputs.

Effect of the key components. We evaluate the effect of the key components of MoRA, containing two objectives and expert approximation. As shown in Table 5, jointly optimizing the LM and diversity losses achieves average scores of 72.17 and 65.30 at 25% and 50% pruning, respectively. Removing the diversity loss reduces these scores by 4.84 and 7.22 points, while removing the LM loss causes drops of 4.69 and 10.73 points. The larger degradation without the LM loss at 50% pruning suggests that predictive utility becomes more important under a tighter retention budget. The diversity objective discourages overlapping routing preferences but does not assess the contribution to next-token prediction of each expert. Consequently, selecting experts solely for diversity may discard predictively useful experts, with fewer retained experts available to compensate for their removal at higher pruning ratios. 

Pruning with learned biases versus adjusted routing probabilities. 

Table 6 shows that computing gate mass from the bias-adjusted router improves average performance over the original gate mass in all six settings. However, ranking experts by adjusted gate mass still underperforms direct bias-based selection, particularly at 50% pruning on Qwen3 and DeepSeek-V2-Lite, where the gaps reach 6.49 and 2.64 percentage points, respectively. These results indicate that aggregating routing probabilities after bias calibration remains less effective than directly ranking experts by the learned biases, particularly at high pruning ratios. This suggests that expert utilization, even under the calibrated router, does not fully capture predictive importance.

# 5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we propose MoRA, a framework for structured MoE expert pruning. We introduce a learnable router bias for each expert that sharpens the routing probability distributions. Experts that are critical to the predictive performance tend to receive higher learned biases and are therefore prioritized for retention. The retained experts are then used to approximate the outputs of the pruned experts through channel-wise affine transformations. Extensive experiments across three MoE architectures demonstrate that MoRA outperforms the evaluated baselines and preserves predictive performance with minimal additional parameter overhead. 11 References
