Abstract 

In collaborative foundation model fine-tuning, client data is rarely homogeneous. Instead, clients typically possess unknown mixtures of distinct data distributions, or tasks . Conventional federated learning primarily addresses heterogeneity across clients without explicitly resolving latent task mixtures within each client. We study this setting as compound heterogeneity , where data is heterogeneous both across and within clients. We study adaptation over a common frozen representation and show that, when tasks share the same feature geometry, the optimal model for a client’s task mixture under squared loss is a convex combination of the optimal models for its underlying tasks. Thus, a single locally trained model represents the client’s overall task mixture, while individual inputs may be drawn from different underlying task distributions. This motivates routing inputs to specialized experts, and we show that, when the task optima form a simplex, task-aligned routing achieves lower risk than any single adapted model for genuinely mixed clients. With access to a small set of task-labeled public samples, we derive a convex program to recover task experts and match them to their corresponding tasks. Our routing analysis shows that effective specialization requires input-dependent expert selection aligned with each client’s task mixture. Motivated by this analysis, we propose FedSEE. Across our experiments, FedSEE avoids the negative transfer observed in the evaluated baselines and improves performance by 2.9 points overall and 3.7 points for the worst-served quartile. 

# 1 Introduction 

Modern learning systems often adapt models using data distributed across users, institutions, or devices. A common distributed-learning abstraction assigns each participant a single local distribution Pi, so non-IID structure arises across participants ( Pi̸ = Pj ) (McMahan et al., 2017; Li et al., 2020; Karimireddy et al., 2020). Yet a participant may itself come from multiple underlying data distributions, such as conversation, code, and prose for a personal assistant or records from different hospital departments. In this regime, heterogeneity exists both across and 

within participants, and each local dataset is a mixture whose composition varies across the population. A latent-mixture view of client distributions has been studied in federated multi-task learning (Marfoq et al., 2021). We term the resulting combined within- and across-client variation compound heterogeneity , modeling each client i’s data distribution Di as an unknown mixture Di = PMm=1 αim Pm of M shared task distributions, where the latent mixture proportions αi ∈ ∆M −1 vary across clients. Specifically, we focus on the regime in which clients are themselves mixed and their mixture proportions differ across the population. For such mixed clients, under a shared feature representation and squared loss, we show that the client optimum is a weighted combination of the task-specific optima, with weights given by the client’s mixture proportions,

This shifts the learning objective from estimating one optimum per client to recovering the underlying task experts and learning to route among them. This naturally leads to a mixture-of-experts (MoE) formulation (Jacobs et al., 1991; Shazeer et al., 2017) in which the task-specific optima define the experts and input-dependent routing determines how they are combined. Recovering these task experts from mixed-client models is not straightforward. When the task optima form a simplex, the task experts correspond to its vertices and mixed-client optima lie within their convex hull. Averaging mixed-client optima therefore remains inside the simplex rather than recovering its vertices, while hard client clustering assigns each mixed client to a single cluster even when its optimum lies between task experts (Figure 1). To recover the task experts, the server optimizes task-specific losses over convex combinations of the pooled client uploads. A small task-labeled public set provides these losses, allowing each extraction problem to target a specific task and yielding a convex recovery program (Theorem 2). The supervision also resolves the identity of the recovered experts. Without task-indexed information, the expert set may still be recovered, but one cannot determine which expert cor-responds to which task (Theorem 3). As a result, experts learned on different clients cannot be reliably matched by task before aggregation. Recovering the task experts is not sufficient for special-ization because each input must also be routed to the expert corresponding to its underlying task. If inputs are routed to their corresponding task experts, the client’s average expert usage naturally reflects its task mixture αi. Our analysis quantifies how both the client’s average expert usage and the input-dependent routing decisions affect the resulting risk (Theorem 5). In particular, enforcing uniform average expert usage is incompatible with exact specialization when a client’s task mixture is non-uniform (Proposition 1). Motivated by this analysis, we propose FedSEE (Feder-ated Supervised Expert Extraction), which combines task-specific LoRA experts (Hu et al., 2022) with supervised extraction and mixture-aware routing. FedSEE shares the LoRA down-projection across experts, isolating task-specific adaptation in the remaining trainable parameters and enabling supervised expert extraction from mixed client updates. The resulting extraction weights indicate how strongly each client contributes to each recovered task expert, providing a proxy for the client’s latent task mixture that guides expert selection and routing calibration. FedSEE also supports client-dependent training capacity by allowing each client to update only a subset of its available experts. We summarize our main contributions as follows. 

• Geometry under task mixtures. We characterize how within-client task mixtures are represented after local optimization and show that, under the stated squared-loss conditions, each client optimum is a mixture-weighted combination of the underlying task optima, inducing a simplex geometry. 

• Expert recovery and identifiability. We derive a task-supervised convex program for recovering task experts from mixed client uploads, together with recovery guarantees. We further characterize when task identities and the expert set itself cannot be determined from the available information. 

• Mixture-aware routing. We characterize two requirements for specialization. Average expert usage should reflect the client’s task mixture, while routing must remain input dependent to specialize individual inputs. 2• FedSEE. We instantiate these principles in a federated LoRA framework that extracts task-specific experts from mixed client updates and performs mixture-aware routing with client-specific calibration. Across our experiments, FedSEE improves over the strongest baseline by 2 .9 points overall and 3 .7 points on the worst-served quartile, and is the only evaluated method to avoid negative transfer across the five-point heterogeneity sweep. 

Closest work. Related approaches span mixture modeling, federated expert construction, and centralized model composition. Mixture-based methods such as FedEM (Marfoq et al., 2021) and FedSoft (Ruan and Joe-Wong, 2022) allow a client’s data to combine multiple latent distributions, but jointly learn the corresponding components during federated optimization rather than recovering task-indexed experts from already mixed client adaptations. Hard clustering methods such as IFCA (Ghosh et al., 2020) instead assign each client to a single component and therefore cannot directly represent clients spanning multiple tasks. Among federated LoRA and mixture-of-experts methods, FedLEASE (Wang et al., 2025) is closest to our setting, but constructs experts through client clustering and within-cluster aggregation rather than task-indexed recovery from mixed uploads. Centralized model and adapter composition methods (Ilharco et al., 2023; Yadav et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2024) assume that task-specific models or adapters are already available and learn how to combine them. FedSEE addresses the inverse problem in which the observed client adaptations are mixtures of latent task experts that must first be recovered and identified. Appendix A provides a detailed comparison.

# 5 Experiments 

Synthetic validation. Under the assumptions of Section 3, controlled experiments instantiate the simplex geometry illustrated in Figure 1. They confirm the main theoretical predictions. Expert-recovery error scales linearly with anchor impurity, task supervision resolves the identity ambiguity of label-free recovery, sufficiently interior upload pools exhibit the non-identifiability of Theorem 4, and mixture-aware routing avoids the cost of uniform calibration. Appendix B.1 provides the full construction and additional results. 

## 5.1 Natural Language Understanding Benchmark 

We evaluate FedSEE on six GLUE/SuperGLUE tasks (Wang et al., 2018, 2019): SST-2, QNLI, CoLA, QQP, WiC, and BoolQ, forming M = 6 tasks. Client task proportions follow a symmetric Dirichlet distribution with concentration αDir , where smaller values produce more concentrated mixtures and larger values approach uniform composition. Each client has disjoint 200-example validation and test sets matched to its realized task mixture. The validation set is used only for model selection, while the test set is held out for final personalized evaluation. FedSEE assumes access to a small task-labeled public reference set, used only for server-side expert extraction in Eq. (13). We use 1 ,000 public examples per task by default, all disjoint from client training, validation, and test data. Appendix B.6 studies sensitivity to the amount of public supervision and shows that the advantage is retained with only 100 labeled examples per task. By default, all six recovered experts are available at inference while each client updates one active expert, denoted FedSEE (L1) . We compare against averaging methods (FedIT (Zhang et al., 2024), FFA-LoRA (Sun et al., 2024)), personalized LoRA methods ( FedSA-LoRA (Guo et al., 2025), FedDPA-T (Yang et al., 2024)), and clustering methods ( IFCA+LoRA (Ghosh et al., 2020),

# 6 Conclusion and Future Work 

We studied personalization under compound heterogeneity, where clients may contain mixtures of latent tasks. Under our shared-feature quadratic model, client optima are mixture-weighted barycenters of task optima, with affine independence inducing a simplex geometry. This exposes limitations of single-adapter aggregation and label-free expert construction, motivating task-supervised expert recovery and mixture-aware, input-dependent routing. FedSEE instantiates these principles with LoRA by extracting task-aligned experts from mixed client updates and routing them using client-specific mixture calibration. On natural language understanding tasks, FedSEE improves average and tail personalization and remains competitive from pure to near-uniform client populations. These results support explicit modeling of within-client mixture structure across heterogeneity regimes. The present analysis suggests two natural directions for extending the framework. First, the exact barycentric characterization holds under the squared-loss model, while the general-loss analysis of Appendix D.6 introduces a perturbation controlled by the variation in task curvature. A sharper characterization of this perturbation, particularly for deep models trained with cross-entropy, would further connect the theoretical framework to practical fine-tuning settings. Second, the framework assumes that the number of underlying tasks M is known. In practical federated settings, this quantity may itself need to be inferred from the client population. Extending supervised expert extraction to jointly estimate the number of latent tasks and their associated experts is an important direction for future work.

Statistical Heterogeneity and Mixture Models. The canonical challenge in federated learning is the divergence of client data distributions. Traditional methods mitigate this via proximal regularization (FedProx (Li et al., 2020)), control variates (SCAFFOLD (Karimireddy et al., 2020)), meta-learning-based personalization (Per-FedAvg (Fallah et al., 2020); pFedMe (T. Dinh et al., 2020)), model interpolation (Deng et al., 2020; Mansour et al., 2020), personalized empirical risk minimization (PERM (Deng et al., 2023)). A related line of work explicitly models each client’s data as a mixture of latent source distributions. FedEM (Marfoq et al., 2021) jointly learns shared latent components and client-specific mixture weights, while FedSoft (Ruan and Joe-Wong, 2022) learns cluster and personalized models under soft client membership. These methods estimate the latent components as part of federated optimization. FedSEE studies a different recovery problem in which local optimization has already produced mixed client adaptations and the server must recover task-indexed experts from the resulting upload pool. Hard clustering methods such as IFCA (Ghosh et al., 2020) instead assign each client to a single component. We evaluate this paradigm as IFCA+LoRA by pairing each cluster with its own dedicated adapter. Without task-indexed side information, latent components remain semantically unlabeled, and without sufficient boundary information even the unordered expert set may be non-identifiable (Theorems 3 and 4). By leveraging task-labeled public data, FedSEE fixes the task correspondence and, under the anchor condition, recovers task-indexed experts with the guarantee of Theorem 2. 

Federated LoRA Fine-Tuning. Early federated PEFT frameworks such as FedIT (Zhang et al., 2024) apply standard federated aggregation to LoRA parameters. Because separately averaging the two LoRA factors does not generally reproduce the average of their products, ¯B ¯A̸ = BA , subsequent methods focus on reducing this aggregation mismatch. FedEx-LoRA (Singhal et al., 2024) corrects the residual aggregation error, FLoRA (Wang et al., 2024) stacks client updates along the rank dimension, FLoRA-NA (Nguyen et al., 2025) constructs surrogate aggregated factors, and FedRot-LoRA (Zhang et al., 2026) aligns equivalent low-rank factorizations before aggregation. These methods target an accurate global adapted model rather than the recovery of task-indexed experts. Other approaches exploit the asymmetric roles of the LoRA factors. FedSA-LoRA (Guo et al., 2025) shares only the A factor, while FFA-LoRA (Sun et al., 2024) fixes one randomly initialized factor and optimizes only the other. FedSEE instead learns a shared projection Aglobal during initialization and then freezes it. Beyond reducing aggregation ambiguity, this common projection gives the theoretical model a shared feature space in which client optima in B-space become mixture-weighted combinations of task optima (Theorem 1). 

Federated Mixture-of-Experts. A complementary line of work preserves heterogeneity through multiple adapters or experts. MoLE (Wu et al., 2024) learns gating functions over a collection of independently trained LoRA experts. In federated settings, FedLEASE (Wang et al., 2025) clusters client representations to construct domain-specific experts and subsequently uses an adaptive mixture-of-experts mechanism to select among them. Related personalized approaches such as FedDPA (Yang et al., 2024) maintain global and local adapters and combine them through instance-dependent weighting. FedSEE differs from clustering-based expert construction in two respects. First, each mixed client upload may contribute fractionally to multiple recovered task experts through the extraction weights and the resulting contribution matrix C. Second, the recovered experts are optimized against task-labeled public risks, which assigns them explicit task identities rather than identities induced by client clusters. Resource-heterogeneous FedLoRA methods such as FlexLoRA (Bai et al., 2024) instead adapt LoRA rank to client capabilities and use SVD-based redistribution of the aggregated update. FedSEE addresses a different form of system heterogeneity by separating the experts available for inference from those a client can actively update through its capacity-aware active/passive partition. 

Centralized Model and Adapter Composition. A parallel non-federated literature studies combining independently trained task-specific models or adapters through weight arithmetic (Task Arithmetic (Ilharco et al., 2023)), conflict resolution (TIES-Merging (Yadav et al., 2023)), orthogonalization (ZipLoRA (Shah et al., 2024); DO-Merging (Zheng et al., 2025)), or modular decomposition (LoRA-LEGO (Zhao et al., 2025)). At the same time, specialization of mixed clients requires input-dependent routing, while mixture-aware calibration controls the router’s average expert usage. 

## B.3 Experimental Setup 

This subsection specifies the data construction, evaluation protocol, baseline implementations, model configurations, and optimization settings used in Section 5. Unless stated otherwise, these settings are held fixed within the corresponding experimental protocol. 

B.3.1 Tasks, Public Data, and Evaluation Task pool. We use six classification tasks from GLUE and SuperGLUE (Wang et al., 2018, 2019): SST-2, QNLI, CoLA, QQP, WiC, and BoolQ. These tasks form a common pool of M = 6 task distributions. The same task pool is used in the headline comparison, heterogeneity sweep, and ablations. 

Public reference data. For each task m, we reserve 1 ,000 labeled examples as the public reference set Dsm.These examples are disjoint from all client training, validation, and test data and are never used for local model training or evaluation. FedSEE uses them only at the server to evaluate the task-indexed public losses required by the supervised extraction program Eq. (13). The evaluated baselines do not use these public examples. The public-set-size ablation varies this resource explicitly to quantify the sensitivity of FedSEE to the amount of server-side supervision. 

Validation and personalized evaluation. Each client is assigned disjoint validation and test sets of 200 examples each, with task compositions matched to its realized training mixture. The validation set is used only for hyperparameter and model selection. The test set is not used for local training, server-side extraction, router calibration, or model selection and is evaluated only after the experimental configuration has been fixed. For each random seed, all compared methods use the same client training, validation, and test partitions. Overall accuracy is the mean of the per-client test accuracies. We additionally report the mean test accuracy of the worst-served 50% and 25% of clients to measure personalization quality away from the population average. 

B.3.2 Compound-Client Construction 

The client partitions are constructed to control both within-client and across-client heterogeneity while keeping the underlying set of tasks fixed. Each task contributes at most 5 ,000 training examples to the allocation pool, or all available examples when its source training set is smaller. In particular, WiC contributes 4 ,428 examples. 22 Pure-client endpoint. The condition denoted αDir = 0 is not sampled from a Dirichlet distribution. It is a deterministic pure-client reference in which every client receives examples from exactly one task. In the N = 12 heterogeneity sweep, two clients are assigned to each of the six tasks. Each task’s available pool is divided between its assigned clients, with any integer-division remainder assigned to the final client. This realizes the vertex regime of the simplex geometry in Section 3. 

Mixed-client regimes. For αDir ∈ { 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 50 }, one task-proportion vector is drawn for each client from the symmetric distribution Dirichlet( αDir 1). The αDir = 50 condition is denoted uniform in the figures and tables because its draws are strongly concentrated around equal task proportions. Smaller concentration parameters produce more peaked and more diverse client mixtures, whereas larger values move clients toward similar, near-uniform compositions. Before converting a sampled mixture into integer data allocations, task weights below 0 .05 are set to zero and the remaining entries are renormalized. This preprocessing rule is applied identically to the client partitions used by all compared methods. 

Supply-constrained allocation. Each client’s mixture vector is scaled by a common nominal sample budget obtained from the total available task supply and the number of clients. This produces a requested number of examples from each task. For a task whose aggregate requests exceed its available supply, all client requests for that task are reduced proportionally until the total matches the available pool. When aggregate demand falls below supply, the unused remainder is left unallocated rather than redistributed. Consequently, the realized amount of training data varies with the sampled client mixtures. Table 7 reports the realized pooled client training–validation allocation at each heterogeneity level.

B.3.3 Baseline Implementations 

We compare against six federated LoRA methods spanning global aggregation, personalization, and clustering. For a given experiment, all methods use the same client partitions, pretrained backbone, communication-round budget, local-epoch budget, batch size, maximum sequence length, LoRA rank, and adapted transformer modules unless the method itself requires a different treatment of the LoRA factors. Method-specific aggregation and personalization follow the respective published procedures. 

FedIT. FedIT (Zhang et al., 2024) applies standard federated averaging to both LoRA factors A and B, producing a single global adapter shared by all clients. 

FFA-LoRA. FFA-LoRA (Sun et al., 2024) keeps the LoRA projection A fixed and aggregates the trainable B

updates, producing a single shared adapter without averaging two independently changing LoRA factors. 23 FedSA-LoRA. FedSA-LoRA (Guo et al., 2025) exploits the asymmetric roles of the two LoRA factors by aggregating A while retaining a personalized client-side B component. 

FedDPA-T. FedDPA-T (Yang et al., 2024) maintains global and personalized adapter components and combines them at inference through its test-time personalization procedure. We use the test-time variant throughout the reported comparisons. 

IFCA+LoRA. We instantiate IFCA (Ghosh et al., 2020) with LoRA by maintaining a separate LoRA adapter for each cluster and iteratively assigning each client to a single cluster using the IFCA hard-assignment procedure. A client updates only the adapter associated with its assigned cluster. 

FedLEASE. FedLEASE (Wang et al., 2025) clusters client B updates using cosine similarity, constructs one expert per cluster, and applies its published routing mechanism over the resulting expert pool. The conceptual relationships among these methods and FedSEE are discussed separately in Appendix A. The description here concerns their experimental instantiation.

## C.3 The Mixture Proxy and Capacity Assignment 

The barycentric analysis of Section 3 motivates a client-specific mixture profile over the recovered experts. FedSEE obtains this profile directly from the client contributions produced by the joint extraction procedure. ## C.4 Router Parameterization and Aggregation 

FedSEE uses a lightweight linear router at each routed layer. For an input token representation x, the router produces expert logits through W ℓr x + bℓr. Experts outside the client-specific inference set Ui are masked before applying the softmax, yielding routing probabilities over the experts available to client i. The KL term in Eq. (14) is applied at each routed layer and averaged over P. Its mean-routing statistic ¯ pℓi is estimated on each minibatch by averaging the routing probabilities over its examples and token positions. The router is optimized with a separate learning rate ηr .After each communication round, the server averages the router parameters across participating clients and broadcasts the resulting shared router for the next round. Each client retains its own inference set Ui and mixture-calibration target ˜ ci, so the shared router is trained under different client-specific masks and calibration targets across the federation. The theoretical justification for using a shared routing function is given in Appendix D.5.

The preceding results distinguish three aspects of expert recovery: recovery accuracy under task supervision, recovery of the unordered vertex set from client uploads, and identification of the expert-to-task correspondence. We next relate the supervised upper bound of Theorem 2 to the two identifiability lower bounds. Theorem 4 addresses recovery of the vertex set from the upload pool alone. Under the γ-interior condition its lower bound is linear in γ whenever γ ≤ sep /(2 diam F(Θ)). Moreover, every γ-interior population has anchor impurity at least ( M − 1) γ/M . Thus the lower bound captures the same linear dependence on distance from the simplex boundary as the anchor term in Theorem 2; for families in which ε = Θ( γ), the two scale identically up to conditioning and simplex geometry. Theorem 4 applies to pool-only recovery. FedSEE’s supervised program uses labeled public loss to identify task directions, while its anchor condition controls the geometric projection of each task vertex onto the convex hull of the uploads (Remark 5). Furthermore, a sharp distinction exists between recovering the vertex set and identifying its task correspondence. Boundary information can make the unordered vertex set recoverable, but it cannot by itself determine which vertex corresponds to which task. As anchor clients approach purity, the supervised recovery error vanishes, whereas the task-indexed label-blind floor of Theorem 3 remains fixed. The task-labeled public loss supplies an identification margin: Proposition 4 shows that the population loss for task m separates its correct vertex from the alternatives by at least 14 σmin (Φ) sep 2. Thus task-indexed side information is necessary to establish the true vertex–task correspondence; in FedSEE, this information is supplied by the labeled public data.

## D.7 Additional Model-Class Instantiations 

The preceding analysis extends the framework along the loss dimension. A complementary question is how the shared-representation abstraction applies beyond the LoRA instantiation used by FedSEE. For the squared-loss results of Section 3, the relevant quantities are determined jointly by the induced representation and the resulting expert geometry: 

• σmin (Φ) and σmax (Φ) characterize the conditioning of the shared feature geometry; 

• h and diam F(Θ) characterize the geometry of the task-expert simplex and enter the recovery and routing guarantees; 57 • Q, the feature bound of Assumption 4, controls the finite-sample and routing-variation terms. Any model whose induced ( f0, ϕ ) representation and task experts satisfy the corresponding assumptions inherits the guarantees of Section 3 with the associated constants. Section 4 develops the LoRA instantiation. We give two additional examples below. 

Linear probing and last-layer personalization. Let ϕ be a frozen pre-trained encoder and let θ denote the trainable linear head. In this case, Assumption 2(i) has the usual interpretation that each task is well specified within the shared representation. Assumption 2(ii) additionally requires the tasks to induce a common feature second moment, which need not hold for a general pre-trained encoder because different task distributions may induce different feature covariances. When these covariances are only approximately shared, the covariance-mismatch analysis of Remark 1 in Appendix D.1 provides the corresponding perturbed interpretation through 

ρΦ.

Random features and fixed kernel representations. Let ϕ be a fixed finite-dimensional random-feature map, or a fixed feature representation associated with a kernel, and let θ denote the corresponding linear coefficient block. Bounded features directly satisfy the representation bound of Assumption 4, while feature non-degeneracy gives the corresponding covariance lower bound. The remaining conditions are not automatic. Task realizability requires the task predictors to lie in the span of the chosen feature map, and Assumption 2(ii) requires the induced feature covariance to be shared across tasks. When the latter holds only approximately, Remark 1 again provides the appropriate covariance-perturbation interpretation. These examples illustrate that the framework is not specific to LoRA, while its guarantees remain conditional on the induced representation and task geometry satisfying the stated assumptions. Directions for relaxing these assumptions are discussed in Section 6.
