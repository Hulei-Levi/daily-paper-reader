Abstract 

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layers are central to frontier language models, and recent architectures push toward more and smaller experts. In this regime, the standard linear router becomes a bottleneck: with 

M experts and hidden dimension h, its per-token cost Θ( M h ) dominates the MoE layer once M is large. We introduce MoRE ( Mixture of Rank-reduced-routed Experts), which factorizes the router weight matrix at rank r and reduces the routing cost to O(( h + M )r). We prove that rank logarithmic in M suffices for routing expressivity when the number of active experts is fixed, and is necessary up to precision factors. We also prove that logarithmic rank preserves load balance in a Gaussian memorization model, and training on a synthetic phonebook task shows that low rank does not hurt memorization. At matched active FLOPs, the factorization allows a factor of Θ( h/r ) more experts. To realize this gain in wall-clock time, we design a fused Triton kernel at inference that avoids expensive memory operations on HBM. Empirically, MoRE improves memorization on the phonebook task and performance on knowledge-intensive Q&A benchmarks after pretraining, while matching reasoning ability. 1

## 1 Introduction 

Mixture-of-experts (MoE) is now the dominant architectural paradigm for frontier language mod-els (DeepSeek-AI, 2024a,b; Jiang et al., 2024; xAI, 2024; Yan et al., 2026). In these architectures, the standard dense MLP is replaced by a pool of independent experts and a router layer that activates only a small subset of experts per token. Recent advances in model scaling rely heavily on increasing the number of experts in MoE layers (Abnar et al., 2025). This approach expands the total parameter count, allowing models to benefit from neural scaling laws without proportionally increasing active compute (Abnar et al., 2025; Kaplan et al., 2020). In particular, at a fixed active compute budget, MoE layers achieve significantly better memorization and factual recall than dense MLPs (Jelassi et al., 2025). Recent frontier MoE models are also increasingly operating in the 

fine-grained regime (Appendix A), in which the layer is divided into a larger number of smaller experts, each with intermediate width below the hidden dimension (Krajewski et al., 2024). However, further scaling the number of experts in MoE layers hits a bottleneck: routing costs begin to dominate the expert cost as the expert pool grows. This is driven by two distinct factors: the increased active compute required to calculate the routing function, and the heavy memory-bandwidth cost of writing and reading the routing scores. 1.1 Our contributions 

In this work, we propose Mixture of Rank-reduced-routed Experts (MoRE), an architectural design that allows for scaling to more experts with an asymptotically lower computational cost. MoRE replaces the full-rank linear router with a low-rank router . This factorization reduces the number of parameters in the routing function and the FLOPs required for a forward pass, allowing scaling to more experts. Our router design is inspired by the low-rank parameterization of key-query (KQ) weights in recent attention architectures (DeepSeek-AI, 2024a) as well as the low-rank design of LoRA adapters (Hu et al., 2021). 

Expressivity guarantees. To determine whether the rank bottleneck limits MoRE’s routing capacity, we study which expert assignments it can realize and how evenly it can use the experts. In Section 3, we first prove general upper and lower bounds on the rank needed to realize any top-k

assignment. Then, we prove that low-rank routing in MoRE layers preserves load-balancing 

2ability , by constructing a router with r = Θ(log M ) that balances expert assignment in a Gaussian memorization model extending that of Jelassi et al. (2025). By spreading inputs across experts, balanced routing helps use the memorization capacity of the whole expert pool. We also show empirically on a synthetic memorization task, Phonebook, that low rank does not hurt load balancing and memorization (Section 3.2). 

Scaling experts efficiently. At matched active FLOPs, a MoRE layer with rank-r routing and hidden dimension h supports a factor of Θ( h/r ) more experts than an MoE layer with the standard router (Section 4.1). However, realizing this gain in wall-clock time requires more than a FLOP reduction: materializing the score tensor in HBM (High Bandwidth Memory) becomes expensive when M is large. In this paper, we focus on inference, since a model is trained once but runs inference on every request after deployment and during RL post-training. We therefore design a fused kernel that keeps scores in on-chip SRAM (Section 4). The kernel computes scores in blocks and retains only the running top-k values and expert indices. Notably, the inference time 

(prefilling) of MoRE matches that of MoE at matched active FLOPs, with a much larger number of experts. 

Experimental validation. Finally, we validate our architecture through pretraining experiments on both the phonebook task and real-world Internet data. We show that MoRE models consistently improve performance in memorization for phonebook task and improve on knowledge-intensive tasks such as TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017) and Natural Questions (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019), compared with standard MoEs at equal active FLOPs. Additionally, we find that MoRE matches performance on reasoning tasks. 

1.2 Related Work 

Routing in MoE layers. The standard router in modern MoE is a learned linear layer that scores all M experts per token (Fedus et al., 2022; Lepikhin et al., 2020; Shazeer et al., 2017). Hash routing 

fixes the assignment to a deterministic hash (Roller et al., 2021), but Clark et al. (2022); Dikkala et al. (2023) show that deterministic assignment underperforms learned routing at scale, motivating the search for parameter-efficient learned routers rather than discarding learning. Boix-Adsera (2025) suggests low-rank routing but evaluates it only in a limited distillation setting. In the M ≪ h

regime, X-MoE (Chi et al., 2022) and L2R (Yang et al., 2026) also introduce low-rank projections, but to address issues in token representations and routing geometry. MoRE instead targets the fine-grained regime where M is large and M ≪ h no longer holds, using low-rank routing to reduce the cost of scoring many experts. He (2024) introduces PEER , a product-key retrieval layer with cost O(h√M ). At matched active FLOPs, MoRE takes much less wall-clock time than PEER (see Appendix B). 

Fine-grained MoE and scaling. Recent architectures are increasingly designed in the fine-grained 

regime, pairing a small expert intermediate dimension s with a large expert count M . Krajewski et al. (2024) introduce fine-grained MoE and show that it outperforms coarse-grained MoE at a fixed training budget, and that higher granularity is out of reach because of the routing cost. Li et al. (2026) confirm through extensive experiments that larger granularity and expert counts result in better performance. Since MoE memorization capacity scales with total rather than active parameters (Jelassi et al., 2025), designs with large M are directly motivated. Nevertheless, these works do not address the routing bottleneck that emerges as M grows, and we introduce MoRE to reduce the routing cost. 

Knowledge storage in language models. Two threads study how language models store factual knowledge. The first measures memorization in standard transformer and MoE architectures: Geva 3et al. (2021) interpret FFN layers as key-value memories, Allen-Zhu & Li (2024) establish knowledge capacity scaling laws for dense LMs, and Jelassi et al. (2025) show that for MoE, memorization capacity scales with total parameters rather than active parameters, directly motivating designs with large M . A second thread replaces the FFN with a learnable key-value lookup: Memory Layers at Scale (Berges et al., 2024) scales product-key retrieval to billions of stored embeddings, Ultra-Sparse Memory (Huang et al., 2025) pushes to ultra-sparse activation patterns, and Cheng et al. (2026) extend retrieval-style storage to conditional access. MoRE is complementary to this thread: it preserves the MLP-expert structure of MoE and inherits the memorization analysis of Jelassi et al. (2025) while reducing only the routing cost. 

Efficient MoE systems. Existing fused MoE kernels target expert dispatch : ScatterMoE (Tan et al., 2024), MegaBlocks (Gale et al., 2023), and follow-up IO-aware kernels (Costin et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025) fuse scatter-gather with the per-expert grouped matmul, but leave open the question of how to implement the router efficiently in the many-expert regime. vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) provides a FusedTopKRouter that fuses top-k selection with softmax normalization, but does not fuse the router computation with top-k. At inference, expert offloading (He et al., 2024; Hwang et al., 2024; Xue et al., 2024) reduces serving cost by moving inactive experts to CPU memory, but does not reduce the router computation. MoRE instead fuses both low-rank matmuls and top-k

selection into one router kernel (Section 4.2).

## 3 Low-rank routing preserves memorization capabilities 

MoRE replaces the standard router with a rank-r factorization, which raises two related theoretical questions. First, can the router still express the needed expert assignments through the rank bottleneck? Second, when there are many inputs to memorize, can the low-rank router still use the experts’ parameters efficiently by balancing the inputs assigned to them? We answer both questions positively in Sections 3.1 and 3.2, and confirm the answers with experiments on the synthetic Phonebook memorization task. 

3.1 Routing expressivity 

Here an assignment is a subset of k experts. We show that, for fixed k, a single router can select every assignment in a given family for some input, with rank growing only logarithmically in the number of experts and assignments. This dependence on the number of assignments is necessary up to a factor depending on numerical precision. 

Setup. The rank-r router has a simple geometric form: the rows of R1 ∈ RM ×r define the M

expert vectors ui ∈ Rr, and each input x ∈ Rh reaches R1 only through its projected representation 

z(x) := R2x ∈ Rr where R2 ∈ Rr×h. The router then selects the k experts with the largest routing scores ⟨ui, z (x)⟩, 1 ≤ i ≤ M . For exposition, throughout this section we normalize the scale by assuming ∥ui∥2 ≤ 1 for every expert vector and ∥z(x)∥2 ≤ 1 for every input x under consideration. 2

3.2 Load balancing and memorization capacity 

Memorization, a core function of MoE layers (Du et al., 2022; Geva et al., 2021; Jelassi et al., 2025), concerns how many facts can be stored, not which expert configurations can be addressed. Extending the Gaussian model of Jelassi et al. (2025), we prove that logarithmic rank does not affect load balancing, and then explain how load balancing relates to memorization capacity. 

Proposition 1 (Gaussian load balancing with logarithmic rank) . Let x1, . . . , x n ∼ N (0 , I h) be independent, and suppose M = 2 r (r = Θ(log 2 M )) with h ≥ r. For any 1 ≤ k ≤ M , there exists a rank-r top-k router with M experts that selects every expert with probability exactly k/M on each example. Thus, the low-rank factorization preserves the load-balanced mechanism of standard MoE, while reducing routing complexity from O(M h ) to O(( M + h) log M ). 

Why balanced load maximizes the number of stored facts. The number of facts an MLP expert can memorize is determined by its parameter count, as shown for two-layer networks (Daniely, 62020) and for language models (Allen-Zhu & Li, 2023, 2024). An MoE layer reaches the total capacity of its experts only under balanced load: an overloaded expert receives more facts than it can memorize, and an underutilized expert leaves its parameters unused. 

Experimental validation with trained models. We verify in training that sufficient rank does not affect memorization, using the phonebook task of Jelassi et al. (2025). ## 4 Scaling experts efficiently with fused implementation of MoRE 

Since logarithmic rank keeps the load balanced and does not hurt memorization (Section 3), we ask: at a fixed active-FLOP budget, how much additional capacity does the low-rank router unlock? Section 4.1 shows a Θ( h/r ) gain in the expert count. 3 The resulting large-M regime creates a memory-bandwidth bottleneck on the routing score tensor, which we resolve with a fused Triton (Tillet et al., 2019) kernel for the router (Section 4.2). 

4.1 Scaling under fixed active-FLOPs 

Fix the active-FLOP budget τ , the hidden dimension h, and the active expert count k, so that all non-MoE components have identical parameter counts across the two settings. We compare MoE( M, k, h, s ) and MoRE( M ′, k, h, s ′, r ) on total MLP parameters P = 3 M hs . The full calculation is in Proposition 2 (Appendix I). The active budget τ decomposes into an expert cost Cekhs and a router cost. For MoE, the router cost is CrM h , i.e. Crh FLOPs per expert. For MoRE, the router cost is CrM ′r + Crhr , where the per-expert term Crr is an h/r reduction over MoE, and Crhr is a fixed projection overhead independent of M ′. Since P = 3 M hs ∝ M s is a product, and the budget trades off linearly between scaling M (via the router) and scaling s (via the experts), P is maximized when the budget is split equally between the router and the experts. 4.2 Realizing the speedup at large M

When the expert count M and the number of tokens N are large, running a top-k over the full (N, M ) routing-score tensor requires writing the tensor to HBM (High Bandwidth Memory, see Appendix E.1) and reading it back, and dominates the routing time (Figure 3, Left). This requires 

fusing operations in addition to the low-rank factorization. Below, we sketch how MoRE can be fused and leave the full details to Appendix F.1. Instead of writing the routing scores for every token to HBM and reading them back to compute the top-k

scores, we compute R1R2x and maintain a running top-k buffer (Figure 3, Left) 5. We find that the low-rank router in MoRE is faster than the standard router across different ranks (Figure 3, Middle). MoE can be fused in a similar way, but we find that its speedup from fusing becomes small when 

h is large, while the speedup for MoRE remains visible, because for MoE the cost of computing 

Rx and maintaining the top-k buffer scales with h instead of r (Figure 3, Right). We also show similar speedups on multiple GPUs with expert and data parallelism, where communication costs are involved (Appendix E.3). 

Wall-clock time at inference in the large-M regime. As shown in Figure 4, the fused MoRE layer outperforms the standard MoE layer across the whole grid, and the speedup grows with M

along every row, reaching 1 .83 to 4 .54 × at M = 8 ,192. Equivalently, at matched wall-clock time, MoRE supports about eight times more experts: at h = 512, the standard layer reaches M = 512 in 6.90 ms, while MoRE reaches M = 4 ,096 in 5 .37 ms, realizing the scaling advantage of Section 4.1 in practice. Fusing also reduces peak memory by up to 14 × for the whole layer (Figure 19). We find that MoE and MoRE have similar decoding speed (see Appendix E.4).

5.2 Real-world pretraining: improved performance on knowledge-intensive tasks 

For the real-world pretraining, we construct a 40B-token mixture dataset following the natural-language pretraining recipe of Jelassi et al. (2025): a mixture of FineWeb-edu (HuggingFaceFW, 2024), Cosmopedia (Ben Allal et al., 2024), and Wikipedia, together with the training sets of the downstream benchmarks, so that the model is familiar with the downstream tasks at test time (Allen-Zhu & Li, 2023; Jelassi et al., 2025). We select two MoE/MoRE pairs with matched active FLOPs by Section 4.1, a third pair with wider experts ( s and s′), and a larger pair with M = 1024 and M ′ = hr · M = 8192, trained with expert parallelism. By construction, MoRE has 5 to 6 × more total parameters. On the first three pairs, MoRE reduces validation perplexity by 7% to 15% (Table 1). 7

We evaluate 0-shot via lm-evaluation-harness (Gao et al., 2023) on knowledge-intensive tasks (TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017), Natural Questions (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019)) and commonsense and science reasoning (HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2020), ARC-Easy (Clark et al., 2018), SciQ (Welbl et al., 2017)). MoRE matches or improves knowledge-intensive performance while preserving commonsense and reasoning scores.

## 6 Conclusion and Discussion 

We introduce MoRE , a principled solution to the MoE routing bottleneck that scales experts by a factor of Θ( hr ) while matching active FLOPs. We provide theoretical guarantees for its expressivity, design a hardware-efficient implementation, and demonstrate improved performance on knowledge-intensive benchmarks such as TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017) and Natural Questions (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019), without degrading reasoning. 

Limitations and future work. Our real-world training experiments are still limited in scale (h ≤ 2048, fewer than 10B total parameters) due to our limited computational budget. We also mainly compare MoE and MoRE under the principle of Section 4.1 for improving memorization, and future work could study the scaling laws of the two architectures. While we find that matching the standard router’s initialization variance can already improve upon the default initialization of R1, R 2 (Appendix D), a LoRA-like (Hu et al., 2021) initialization might improve performance further.

## B Additional Discussions in Related Work 

Low-rank routing in the M ≪ h regime. X-MoE (Chi et al., 2022) scores experts by the dot product between a hidden vector x ∈ Rh and M learned expert embeddings. The gradient pulls x toward the span of those embeddings, so after training the hidden states entering a routed layer concentrate in a subspace of Rh of dimension at most M . When M ≪ h, that subspace is much smaller than Rh, a phenomenon the authors call representation collapse . To mitigate this, Chi et al. (2022) project both expert embeddings and the hidden vector to a lower dimension. L2R (Yang et al., 2026) identifies a related but distinct issue: when M ≪ h, token representations and expert embeddings become nearly orthogonal, which narrows the margin between routing logits and produces ambiguous expert selection. L2R introduces low-rank routing to improve discrimination among experts. Our work also uses low-rank routing and derives upper and lower bounds on the rank based on the margin argument, but it serves a different purpose. The mechanisms above require M ≪ h (e.g., 

h = 4096 with M ≤ 64, as in Yang et al. (2026) and Mixtral (Jiang et al., 2024)). MoRE instead targets the fine-grained regime where M is scaled up and M ≪ h does not hold. In this regime, the low-rank router reduces routing cost rather than correcting the geometry of token representations. PEER (He, 2024) reaches a very large expert pool by shrinking each expert to a single neuron and replacing the linear router with product-key retrieval, which has complexity O(h√M ) and is asymptotically better than our O(( M + h)r) where r = Ok(log M ). However, in the following comparison, we find that MoRE improves upon PEER based on the wall-clock speed in practice, with matched active FLOPs.

## D Initialization 

The Switch Transformer (Fedus et al., 2022) sets the router weights elementwise to N (0 , σ 2) with 

σ = 0 .02, and prior work has reported that the precise router initialization variance has limited impact on standard-router performance. The natural default for the low-rank router carries the same per-entry standard deviation over to both factors, i.e. each entry of R1 ∈ RM ×r and R2 ∈ Rr×h

is drawn i.i.d. from N (0 , σ 2) with the same σ = 0 .02. We refer to this as the default (or standard )initialization. Empirically, however, we find that the default initialization noticeably degrades validation perplexity and shifts the optimal router learning rate significantly. We instead choose σ1, σ 2 so that the variance of the router logits matches the standard baseline. Lemma 1 makes this matching condition precise.

## E Efficiency 

For the MoE architecture, we build upon ScatterMoE (Tan et al., 2024) and implement a new fused Triton kernel for the router (refer to Figure 9 for the detailed operations in the forward pass). We believe that further speedups are achievable on specific hardware by applying more careful optimization as in Guo et al. (2025). 

Why we build on ScatterMoE. Figure 9 illustrates the operations in one MoE layer forward pass. Beyond the router, the expert MLP involves token dispatch (sorting by expert assignment), two grouped linear transformations with a SwiGLU activation, and a scatter-back step that combines expert outputs. Each of these steps is a potential source of memory traffic. For example, a naive grouped MoE implementation pays O(N kh ) memory traffic to materialize the gathered tokens (sorted by expert assignment) before the per-expert grouped GEMM, and pays the same again on the scatter-back. ScatterMoE (Tan et al., 2024) fuses scatter-gather into the per-expert grouped matmul, removing both round-trips and producing a kernel whose only HBM traffic is the input X, the weight stack, and the output Y . With this dispatch overhead eliminated, the standard router’s O(N hM ) matmul is the only remaining cost that grows linearly with M . Our low-rank router is designed precisely to address this last bottleneck, while reusing the rest of ScatterMoE’s machinery unchanged. 

E.1 GPU Memory Hierarchy and Related Analysis 

Bandwidth tiers. Modern GPU architectures expose a multi-level memory hierarchy with sharply varying bandwidth. Off-chip high-bandwidth memory (HBM) provides the largest capacity but the lowest bandwidth. On-chip, the L2 cache offers an intermediate level, while each streaming multiprocessor (SM) contains a small pool of SRAM (registers and shared memory) with the highest bandwidth. Any data that does not fit in on-chip memory must be materialized to HBM, incurring an order-of-magnitude bandwidth penalty. 27 Our fusing avoids writing the full ( N, M ) logits and the intermediate tensors of low-rank router to HBM, and computes the Top-K operation in SRAM, which is much faster than the HBM round-trip. 

Comparison to other fused-MoE kernels. Existing efficient MoE kernels, ScatterMoE (Tan et al., 2024), MegaBlocks (Gale et al., 2023), MoMoE (Costin et al., 2025) and SonicMoE (Guo et al., 2025), do top-k computation on the materialized ( N, M ) logits on the HBM and have to pay expensive read and write costs. Our kernel fuses the router with top-k and do sequential top-k

operation on each tile, this avoid expensive memory operations between HBM and SRAM, but the cost would also scale with k, making large k infeasible in our setup. 

E.2 Top-K Algorithm Analysis 

Our implementation is faster than torch.topk when K < 16 and is common across frontier model choices (Table 3). But it is slower than torch.topk for K ≥ 16. For our fused kernel, the per-token cost is Θ( K · M ) + Θ( K2 · M/ BLOCK_M ). For each of the M/ BLOCK_M tiles, we run K iterations to find the K largest values and insert them to the K-element running buffer. The Θ( K · M ) term aggregates the K iterations of max/argmax reductions over the BLOCK_M new candidates, across M/ BLOCK_M tiles. The Θ( K2 · M/ BLOCK_M )term is for computing the minimum over the K-element buffer that happen inside the same K

iterations. We suspect that a more carefully engineered fused topk along the lines of SonicMoE (Guo et al., 2025) (Section D and Appendix F.4), could outperform torch.topk at larger K and close this gap, which we leave to future work. 

E.3 Expert Parallelism 

We explained how MoRE can be implemented efficiently on a single GPU, here we describe how we implement it under expert parallelism, a common setting in large-scale MoE training. We consider there are E GPUs where each GPU contains M/E experts, for simplicity we assume all GPUs are inside one node, and we defer inter-node communication analysis to future work. Following popular design choices (Fedus et al., 2022; Lepikhin et al., 2020; Yan et al., 2026), we replicate non-expert components including router in all GPUs, which our fusing technique still carries over as every GPU has a local router. We could also shard the router across GPUs, i.e. each GPU holds M/E experts and a 1 /E fraction of the router, by computing a top-k locally inside each GPU and then a global top-k across GPUs, which is a hierarchical top-k which we leave to future work. It has the same scaling argument as in the main paper.
