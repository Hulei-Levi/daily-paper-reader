ABSTRACT 

While Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models effectively scale model capacity through sparse activation, their deployment is often bottlenecked by prohibitive memory requirements. Extracting a compact subset of experts presents a promising solu-tion. However, existing expert selection heuristics predominantly rely on Top-k

ranking, which isolates the evaluation of individual experts and ignores the in-tricate inter-expert dependencies introduced by the MoE gating network. In this paper, we propose DS-MoE, a theoretically grounded framework that redefines expert selection via difference-of-submodular (DS) optimization. By analyzing the second-order Taylor expansion of the loss degradation, we reveal functional duality within expert combinations: redundancy (where experts encode overlapping repre-sentations) and synergy (where experts provide complementary error cancellation). To navigate this duality, we mathematically decouple redundancy reduction from synergy maximization by formulating the selection objective as a DS function. Furthermore, we devise a tailored majorization-minimization (MM) algorithm with provable guarantees to efficiently identify the optimal expert subset. Extensive experiments demonstrate that DS-MoE effectively preserves indispensable expert combinations, achieving superior performance compared to the state-of-the-art baselines. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

The pursuit of scaling laws in large language models (LLMs) Kaplan et al. (2020); Shi et al. (2024); Hoffmann et al. (2022); Clark et al. (2022) has driven a fundamental paradigm shift from dense neural networks to Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures Shazeer et al. (2017); Liu et al. (2024b); Fedus et al. (2022); Fang et al. (2026b). In contrast to traditional training paradigms that activate all parameters for every input, the MoE architecture is built on the principle of conditional computation. It relies on a gating mechanism to dynamically route each input token to a sparse subset of experts (e.g., Top-k) Fedus et al. (2022). This selective execution effectively decouples model capacity from computational cost, allowing MoE architectures to scale to the trillion-parameter regime Lepikhin et al. (2021) and unlock massive knowledge capacity without a proportional increase in computing overhead Li et al. (2025); Liu et al. (2024a). Despite its significance, the theoretical understanding of expert contributions to training performance remains in its infancy. Existing expert selection Zhou et al. (2022); Puigcerver et al. (2024); Tan et al. (2024) heuristics evaluate experts in strict isolation, assigning routing probabilities independently and thus ignoring intricate inter-expert dependencies Chi et al. (2022), as detailed in Appendix B. Theoretically, this strict independence assumption equates to approximating the loss landscape’s Hessian matrix as purely diagonal. This oversight inevitably leads to sub-optimal routing: redundant experts monopolize capacity, while combinatorially indispensable but individually negligible synergistic components are discarded. Therefore, expert selection must transcend heuristic scalar sorting and be formulated as a rigorous optimization problem. However, transitioning from isolated scoring to collective evaluation inherently introduces an intractable combinatorial optimization problem. Constrained by the immense discrete space, prior works inevitably default to simplistic and retreat to independent scoring heuristics Zhou et al. (2022); Puigcerver et al. (2024); Tan et al. (2024). Thus, there is a critical need for a framework that can systematically optimize inter-expert synergy and mitigate redundancy without succumbing to combinatorial explosion. To bridge this gap, we propose DS-MoE, a theoretically grounded MoE framework that redefines expert selection via difference-of-submodular (DS) optimization Iyer & Bilmes (2012). As depicted in Figure 1, DS-MoE anchors its selection criterion in the second-order Taylor expansion of the loss degradation, effectively bridging the gap between continuous loss curvature and discrete expert selection. This formulation reveals that the MoE Hessian serves as a precise record of expert inter-dependence, manifesting fundamental duality Hassibi & Stork (1992): positive off-diagonal entries correspond to redundancy (penalizing overlapping functionality), while negative entries capture syn-ergy (rewarding complementary error cancellation). To harness this duality, we decouple redundancy reduction from synergy maximization by formulating the selection objective as a DS optimization problem. Subsequently, we devise a tailored majorization-minimization (MM) algorithm Hunter & Lange (2004); Mairal (2013) with provable guarantees to efficiently identify the optimal expert combination. Our contributions are summarized as follows: i) We theoretically prove that the MoE Hessian is a rigorous metric for characterizing inter-expert interdependence, where its off-diagonal entries precisely quantify the inter-expert functional redundancy and synergy; ii) We cast the key parameter optimization of expert selection as a DS problem, thereby decomposing the collective impact of expert combinations into a controllable trade-off between penalizing redundancy and rewarding synergy; iii) We develop a tailored MM algorithm with guaranteed monotonic convergence to efficiently identify the optimal expert subset; iv) Extensive experiments demonstrate that DS-MoE consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines, and strikes a superior accuracy-efficiency trade-off.

Limitations of Top-k mechanism. While the Top-k mechanism effectively filters experts based on gating logits, it relies on a limiting independence assumption: the utility of an expert is evaluated solely via its isolated routing score Gi(x). This greedy heuristic introduces a critical blind spot regarding inter-expert dependencies Chi et al. (2022). Specifically, it fails to distinguish between functional redundancy and synergy. Consequently, the standard routing protocol risks discarding experts that appear individually negligible but are combinatorially indispensable. By reducing expert selection to a series of independent scalar decisions rather than a combinatorial optimization Puigcerver et al. (2024), current routing protocols fail to exploit the complementary interactions required to drive loss reduction beyond the capacity of isolated experts.

Necessity of Second-Order Curvature. While Eqn. equation 5 incorporates the first-order gradient term, relying solely on this term is insufficient for expert selection due to the vanishing gradient problem on convergence. In a dynamic training regime, experts naturally settle into local stationary points, causing their first-order gradients to diminish ( ∥∇ mL∥ → 0). This induces a severe lineariza-tion error: the approximation fits a flat tangent plane to the loss landscape, failing to capture the steep curvature penalties associated with dropping critical experts. To illustrate this limitation, we consider a simplified MoE landscape ∆L(m) = 100( m1 − 1) 2 + ( m2 − 1) 2, as shown in Figure 2. At the fully activated state (1 , 1) , the gradient vanishes ( ∇mL = 0), rendering the first-order term completely uninformative and incorrectly implying that both experts are equally expendable. For this reason, directly evaluating the impact of expert removal reveals a significant disparity: removing expert 1 (high curvature) incurs a substantial loss penalty of 100(0 − 1) 2 = 100 , whereas removing expert 2 (low curvature) yields a marginal penalty of only 1. Since the Hessian matrix H explicitly encapsulates this critical curvature distinction, retaining the second-order term in Eqn. equation 5 is imperative for establishing a robust selection criterion across all convergence states.

3.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Tasks and Datasets. We conduct extensive experiments across three tasks: i) Natural Language Understanding on AGNews Zhang et al. (2015) dataset; ii) Commonsense Reasoning on PIQA Bisk et al. (2020) and HellaSwag Zellers et al. (2019) datasets; and iii) Advanced Multi-domain Reasoning 

on MMLU Hendrycks et al. (2021) dataset. Comprehensive dataset descriptions and experimental setups are detailed in Appendix D.2. 

Models. We evaluate the proposed DS-MoE framework on three representative large-scale MoE architectures: Switch-Base-32 , Switch-Base-64 Fedus et al. (2022), and DeepSeek-MoE-16B Dai et al. (2024). The implementation details are shown in Appendix D.3. 

Baselines. We compare DS-MoE against four representative baselines under consistent cardinality constraints: i) SEER-MoE Muzio et al. (2024): A state-of-the-art sparse expert efficiency framework that dynamically enforces expert sparsity by introducing a differentiable regularization penalty on the gating distribution; ii) DiEP Bai et al. (2025): A expert selection strategy that utilizes gradient-based evaluation to dynamically identify and drop redundant experts with non-uniform sparsity across layers; iii) Standard MoE (S-MoE) Shazeer et al. (2017): The conventional routing mechanism that independently activates a fixed number of experts for each token based purely on the highest localized gating probabilities; and iv) Random MoE (Rand-MoE): A data-independent protocol where the subset of active experts is sampled uniformly at random during model training, strictly adhering to the required cardinality constraints without utilizing any routing probabilities or gradient signals. 3.2 MAIN RESULTS

Study on the Impact of Hessian Matrix. Figures 3(a)-3(d) compare a purely first-order gradient proxy (w/o Hessian) against our framework utilizing the second-order Hessian matrix (w/ Hessian). The results demonstrate that integrating the Hessian matrix consistently yields higher test accuracy and a sharp reduction in convergence rounds. This validates a fundamental limitation of first-order heuristics: they fail to capture loss landscape curvature and suffer from vanishing gradients in flat regions. In contrast, formulating expert selection via the second-order Hessian explicitly captures combinatorial inter-expert dependencies, enabling accurate evaluation of expert synergies and circumventing sub-optimal local minima. 

Study on the Impact of DS Decomposition. Figures 3(e)-3(h) evaluates the necessity of the DS decomposition by comparing the complete framework ( H) against isolated variants employing strictly the redundancy penalty ( H+) or synergy reward ( H−). The results reveal that deploying either component individually yields marginal performance gains, integrating both via the complete DS decomposition consistently achieves the highest test accuracy and fastest convergence. This confirms that mathematically decoupling redundancy reduction from synergy maximization is indispensable for fully exploiting inter-expert dependencies and optimizing routing efficacy. 

Study on the Impact of MM Algorithm. Figures 3(i)-3(l) evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed MM algorithm against a standard local greedy search strategy (Greedy). We see that the greedy approach is highly susceptible to sub-optimal local minima, manifesting as degraded test accuracy and prolonged convergence times. Conversely, the MM algorithm consistently achieves superior accuracy while significantly reducing the required training rounds. These empirical gains validate the monotonic convergence guarantees intrinsic to the MM framework. By bounding the highly non-convex objective via tight surrogate functions, the MM algorithm can circumvent the sub-optimal routing inherent to heuristic greedy searches. 

4 CONCLUSION 

In this paper, we introduced DS-MoE, a theoretically grounded MoE selection framework. By leveraging the Hessian of the loss degradation, we quantify complex inter-expert dependencies, revealing fundamental duality between functional redundancy and cooperative synergy. To effectively harness this duality, we formulate the expert selection process as a DS optimization problem and develop a tailored MM algorithm with a provable convergence guarantee. 13 B RELATED WORK AND RESEARCH GAP 

The MoE architecture has emerged as a pivotal paradigm for scaling model capacity without incurring proportional computational overhead via sparse expert activation. Recent advancements in MoE primarily focus on routing optimizations and structural refinements: For routing optimizations , existing methods are categorized into token-level Shazeer et al. (2017); Fedus et al. (2022); Lepikhin et al. (2021); Jiang et al. (2024) and expert-level routing Zhou et al. (2022); Puigcerver et al. (2024); Tan et al. (2024). Token-level routing, pioneered by Sparsely-Gated MoE Shazeer et al. (2017), employs a Top-k mechanism to dispatch each token to an expert combina-tion based on localized routing probabilities; Switch Transformer Fedus et al. (2022) streamlined this mechanism by routing each token to only the Top-1 expert, thereby improving training efficiency; GShard Lepikhin et al. (2021) introduced automatic sharding to enable conditional computation in LLMs; Mixtral Jiang et al. (2024) demonstrated the scalability of a sparse Top-2 routing strategy within open-weight foundation models. For expert-level routing, expert choice routing Zhou et al. (2022) inverted this logic by allowing experts to selectively pull their top-scoring tokens up to a predefined capacity for enforcing strict load balancing; Soft-MoE Puigcerver et al. (2024) addressed discrete routing instabilities by allowing experts to pull continuous, weighted combinations of tokens; ScatterMoE Tan et al. (2024) optimized the expert choice mechanism by eliminating padding and redundant memory accesses during token aggregation. For structural refinements , several frameworks have been proposed to enhance system execution efficiency: He et al. (2021); Gale et al. (2023); Zoph et al. (2022); Muzio et al. (2024); Dai et al. (2024): FastMoE He et al. (2021) accelerated training by optimizing communication primitives and customized routing operators; MegaBlocks Gale et al. (2023) utilized block-sparse matrix operations to support variable-capacity routing without token-dropping overhead; ST-MoE Zoph et al. (2022) incorporated auxiliary router z-loss to stabilize routing dynamics; SEER-MoE Muzio et al. (2024) introduced differentiable sparsity regularizers to refine gating distributions; and DeepSeek-MoE Dai et al. (2024) advocated for finer-grained expert partitioning combined with isolated shared experts to capture generalized knowledge. While the aforementioned architectural and routing innovations Shazeer et al. (2017); Fedus et al. (2022); Lepikhin et al. (2021); Jiang et al. (2024); Zhou et al. (2022); Puigcerver et al. (2024); Tan et al. (2024); He et al. (2021); Gale et al. (2023); Zoph et al. (2022); Muzio et al. (2024); Dai et al. (2024) significantly improve MoE efficiency, they share a fundamental limitation: experts are evaluated and selected in absolute isolation. Whether governed by localized gating probabilities or global sparsity regularizers, existing frameworks Zhou et al. (2022); Puigcerver et al. (2024); Tan et al. (2024) compute selection scores independently for each individual expert. Consequently, these approaches inherently fail to distinguish between functional redundancy (i.e., experts encoding overlapping representation subspaces) and synergy (i.e., specific expert combinations providing complementary error compensation). To bridge this gap and explicitly model inter-expert relationships, a rigorous analytical framework is essential. In the broader context of neural network compression, quantifying such interdependencies via second-order (Hessian) information has a well-established theoretical foundation LeCun et al. (1989); Hassibi & Stork (1992). However, adapting Hessian-aware analysis to effectively quantify the complicated combinatorial dependencies within MoE architectures remains a critical, yet unexplored frontier.

D.2 DATASETS AND EVALUATION METRICS 

Datasets. To evaluate the proposed DS-MoE framework against the state-of-the-art routing baselines, we conduct comprehensive empirical evaluations across four widely adopted datasets spanning a spectrum from fundamental semantic comprehension to advanced, knowledge-intensive reasoning. The selected benchmarks evaluate the MoE architectures across varying degrees of complexity and domain heterogeneity: • AGNews (Natural Language Understanding) Zhang et al. (2015): A fundamental bench-mark for text classification comprising news articles categorized into four topical domains (World, Sports, Business, Sci/Tech). We utilize this dataset to evaluate the model’s capac-ity to capture linearly separable semantic representations, verifying whether the routing mechanism can distinctly allocate experts to non-overlapping topics without representation collapse. • PIQA (Physical Interaction Question Answering) Bisk et al. (2020): A physical common-sense reasoning benchmark requiring the deduction of the most plausible continuation of a scenario governed by real-world physical laws. This task challenges the MoE framework to dynamically activate expert subsets specialized in intuitive physics and spatial-temporal logic, extending significantly beyond static factoid retrieval. • HellaSwag (Predictive Reasoning) Zellers et al. (2019): A challenging dataset for evalu-ating grounded, adversarial natural language reasoning. Because the incorrect options in HellaSwag are adversarially generated by machines to be structurally coherent but logi-cally flawed, this benchmark explicitly stresses the MoE’s ability to disentangle spurious correlations, explicitly highlighting the necessity of synergistic expert combinations (error cancellation) to effectively resolve complex logical reasoning. • MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding) Hendrycks et al. (2021): A comprehensive benchmark designed to evaluate zero-shot and few-shot knowledge-intensive reasoning across 57 distinct academic and professional disciplines (e.g., STEM, humanities, social sciences). Given its extreme domain heterogeneity, MMLU is critical for stress-testing the MoE architecture. It validates the MoE framework’s capacity to retain a diverse expert pool and orchestrate combinatorial expert collaboration, while effectively mitigating catastrophic forgetting and negative transfer. 

Evaluation Metrics. To capture both the final generalization capability and the underlying optimiza-tion dynamics, we assess the models using two primary quantitative metrics: • Test Accuracy (%): The standard Top-1 accuracy measured on the unseen test splits across all evaluated tasks. As the primary proxy for generalization efficacy, higher test accuracy 24 provides direct empirical validation that the proposed expert selection successfully preserves indispensable, synergistic expert representations while discarding redundant ones. • Convergence Efficiency (Rounds): Measured by the number of training iterations required for the validation performance to reach a stationary accuracy threshold. Standard Top-k rout-ing often suffers from slow convergence or oscillatory behavior due to conflicting gradient updates from functionally redundant experts. By tracking the optimization trajectory, this metric quantitatively demonstrates how the proposed framework’s mitigation of redundancy translates to accelerated gradient flow and superior training stability. D.3 MODEL ARCHITECTURES AND HYPERPARAMETER SETTINGS 

To conduct the evaluation of the DS-MoE framework, we benchmark our approach against three representative MoE architectures: Switch-Base-32, Switch-Base-64, and DeepSeek-MoE-16B. The detailed model architecture and experimental configurations are outlined below. 

Model Architectures 

• Switch-Base Variants ( N ∈ { 32 , 64 }): Both Switch-Base-32 and Switch-Base-64 models adopt a symmetric sequence-to-sequence architecture, comprising 12 encoder layers and 12 decoder layers. We uniformly set the hidden dimension to dmodel = 768 and the vocabulary size to 32,128. These variants employ an alternating arrangement of dense and sparse layers: specifically, half of the Transformer blocks utilize standard dense feed-forward networks (FFN) with an expansion dimension of 3,072, while the remaining blocks are replaced by sparse MoE modules integrating 32 (resp. Switch-Base-32) and 64 (resp. Switch-Base-64) experts. To ensure computational equivalence for a single forward pass, the internal expansion dimension of all individual experts is strictly locked at 3,072, ensuring parity with the standard dense FFN layers. • DeepSeek-MoE-16B ( N = 64 ): We instantiate the DeepSeek-MoE-16B as a 28-layer decoder-only autoregressive architecture. We set the hidden dimension dmodel = 2 , 048 

and the vocabulary size to 102,400. To stabilize shallow-layer feature representations, the foundational layer incorporates a standard dense FFN with an intermediate dimension of 10,944. The subsequent 27 layers are configured as MoE modules. Within each MoE layer, we deploy a hybrid expert configuration consisting of 64 independent routed experts with an individual dimension of 1,408. 

Experimental Configurations 

• Optimization and Parameter Updating Strategy: All experiments are executed using bfloat16 mixed precision and the 8-bit AdamW optimizer. The learning rate is uniformly set to 1 × 10 −4 across all tasks. We configure a micro-batch size of 4 coupled with 4 gradient accumulation steps, yielding a batch size of 16. During fine-tuning, only the sparse components (expert networks and routers) and task-specific heads are updated, while the dense backbone remains frozen. • Task-Specific Setup: We adapt the maximum sequence length based on the inherent context requirements of each specific benchmark. Specifically, the lengths are configured to 128 tokens for AGNews; 256 tokens for PIQA; and 512 tokens for HellaSwag and MMLU. During the evaluation of generative multiple-choice tasks (such as HellaSwag and MMLU), the generation configuration is strictly bounded to a maximum of 4 new tokens. 

Specific Configurations for MM Algorithm. The proposed MM expert selection framework introduces several algorithm-specific hyperparameters. We set the maximum iteration threshold of 

Tmax = 10 and active expert capacity k = 2 by default unless specified otherwise.

F PRACTICAL IMPACT 

By bridging rigorous theoretical guarantees with expert selection optimizations, the DS-MoE frame-work addresses critical bottlenecks in the training and deployment of large-scale MoE architectures: • Theoretically Grounded Routing Paradigm: The training of industrial-scale MoE mod-els is impeded by routing collapse and optimization instabilities, necessitating extensive hyperparameter tuning. By introducing an MM framework with guaranteed monotonic convergence, DS-MoE circumvents the heuristic unpredictability inherent in standard Top-k

routing, thereby enhancing the training performance. • Facilitating Deployment in Resource-Constrained Edge Systems: By optimizing repre-sentational capacity under strict cardinality constraints (e.g., a predefined expert budget k), DS-MoE drastically reduces the memory footprint and computing requirements for training and deploying LLMs. This enables resource-constrained computing entities to leverage the state-of-the-art MoE architectures without compromising performance. • Sustainable and Cost-Effective Training: As demonstrated in our empirical results, decoupling functional redundancy from expert synergy resolves gradient conflicts and substantially expedites model convergence. This algorithmic efficiency translates directly to significant reductions in computing overhead and overall energy consumption, rendering the training of large-scale MoE models highly resource-efficient and economically viable. 26
